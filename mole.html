The mole

A mole is just a counting word, like dozen. It lets you count atoms by weighing them.

  • Say what a mole is, in plain words
  • Find the molar mass of any compound
  • Convert between grams, moles and particles
  • Count the atoms inside a molecule

What is a mole?

Atoms are far too small to count one at a time, and too small to weigh one at a time. So chemists invented a giant counting word. Step through the story below.

One mole of anything is $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ of that thing. That number is called Avogadro's number.

A dozen eggs and a dozen elephants have the same count but very different weights. Moles work the same way. Yes, I am named after one. No, I have not counted to it.

Same count, different weight

Here is the part that makes moles useful. Every substance has a molar mass: how many grams one mole of it weighs. Change the substance and the number of particles stays the same, but the scale moves.

Finding molar mass

You never need to memorize molar masses. Add up the atomic masses from the periodic table, one for every atom in the formula. The number on the table in atomic mass units is the same number in grams per mole.

Exam trap: parentheses

A number after parentheses multiplies everything inside them. $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ has 2 O and 2 H, not 1 O and 2 H. The molar mass is $40.08 + 2(16.00) + 2(1.008) = 74.10\text{ g/mol}$.

The mole bridge

Almost every stoichiometry problem starts by moving between three islands. Moles sit in the middle. There is no direct bridge between grams and particles, so you always have to pass through moles.

The two conversions you need

$$\text{moles} = \frac{\text{grams}}{\text{molar mass}} \qquad \text{particles} = \text{moles} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}$$

Worked example: grams to molecules

How many molecules are in 36.0 g of water?

  1. Find the molar mass. $\ce{H2O}$: $2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.02\text{ g/mol}$.
  2. Cross the first bridge, grams to moles. Write it so grams cancel: $36.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{18.02\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 2.00\text{ mol}$.
  3. Cross the second bridge, moles to molecules: $2.00\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ molecules}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}}$.
  4. The answer is $1.20 \times 10^{24}$ molecules. Three significant figures, because 36.0 has three.

Counting atoms inside molecules

A favorite exam twist: asking for atoms when you have molecules. One molecule of $\ce{H2O}$ holds 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. So one mole of $\ce{H2O}$ holds 2 moles of hydrogen atoms and 1 mole of oxygen atoms. The subscript is a ratio that works at every scale.

Worked example: atoms in a compound

How many oxygen atoms are in 0.500 mol of $\ce{CO2}$?

  1. Read the subscript: each $\ce{CO2}$ holds 2 O atoms, so 1 mol of $\ce{CO2}$ holds 2 mol of O atoms.
  2. Find the moles of O: $0.500\text{ mol CO}_2 \times \dfrac{2\text{ mol O}}{1\text{ mol CO}_2} = 1.00\text{ mol O}$.
  3. Convert to atoms: $1.00\text{ mol} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = $ $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ O atoms.

Exam trap: more grams does not mean more stuff

Which has more atoms, 10 g of hydrogen or 10 g of gold? Students often call it a tie. It is not close. Hydrogen is about 195 times lighter per atom, so 10 g of hydrogen holds about 195 times as many atoms. To compare amounts, always convert to moles first.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • A mole is a count: $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ of anything.
  • Molar mass is grams per mole. Add up the atomic masses in the formula.
  • Grams to moles: divide by molar mass. Moles to particles: multiply by Avogadro's number.
  • You cannot jump from grams to particles without passing through moles.
  • Subscripts tell you how many moles of each atom are in one mole of compound.
