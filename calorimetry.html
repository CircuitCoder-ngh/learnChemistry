On every exam

Calorimetry

You cannot see heat, but you can catch it in water and read a thermometer. That is all a calorimeter is.

  • Explain why heat lost equals heat gained
  • Find the final temperature when hot metal meets cool water
  • Turn a coffee cup measurement into $\Delta H$ per mole, with the right sign
  • Use a bomb calorimeter and say what it measures

Heat lost equals heat gained

Pour hot coffee into cold milk and you get warm coffee. No one has to tell the heat where to go. It flows from hot to cold until they match. A calorimeter is a container that keeps this heat from escaping, so you can measure it. The simplest one is two foam cups.

Nothing gets out of a good calorimeter, so the heat the hot thing loses is exactly the heat the cold thing gains.

Heat balance

$$\begin{gathered}q_{\text{lost}} + q_{\text{gained}} = 0 \\ m_1 c_1 \Delta T_1 = -\,m_2 c_2 \Delta T_2\end{gathered}$$
$\Delta T$
$T_{\text{final}} - T_{\text{initial}}$ for that object. The hot object gets a negative $\Delta T$.
$T_{\text{final}}$
the same for both objects. That is what "reach equilibrium" means.

The coffee cup lab

Try it. Drop a hot metal into water, or run a reaction right in the water. The whole experiment lasts seconds, and the live graph shows why the numbers come out the way they do.

Yes, real chemists really use foam coffee cups. They are cheap and they insulate well. No coffee is involved. I checked, sadly.

Finding the final temperature

Think of each object's heat as a block. Its height is $mc$, which says how hard it is to warm. Its width is the temperature change. Area is heat, so the two blocks must have the same area. Water is a tall block, so it needs very little width. That is why the final temperature lands near the water's.

Worked example: final temperature

A 50.0 g iron block at 100.0 °C goes into 100.0 g of water at 20.0 °C. What is the final temperature? For iron, $c = 0.45$ J/g·°C.

  1. Write the balance: $q_{\text{iron}} + q_{\text{water}} = 0$.
  2. Fill in $q = mc\Delta T$. Both objects end at the same unknown $T_f$: $(50.0)(0.45)(T_f - 100.0) + (100.0)(4.184)(T_f - 20.0) = 0$.
  3. Multiply out: $22.5\,T_f - 2250 + 418.4\,T_f - 8368 = 0$.
  4. Collect the $T_f$ terms on one side and the numbers on the other: $440.9\,T_f = 10618$.
  5. Divide both sides by 440.9: $T_f = 24.1\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Check: it lies between 20 and 100, and it is close to the water's starting temperature. Good.

Worked example: identify a metal

A 60.0 g metal at 95.0 °C goes into 100.0 g of water at 20.0 °C. The final temperature is 28.6 °C. Find the specific heat of the metal.

  1. Start with the water: $q_{\text{water}} = (100.0\ \text{g})(4.184\ \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}})(28.6 - 20.0\ {}^\circ\text{C}) = +3598\ \text{J}$.
  2. The metal lost that much heat, so $q_{\text{metal}} = -3598\ \text{J}$.
  3. Find the metal's change: $\Delta T = 28.6 - 95.0 = -66.4\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
  4. Solve for $c$. Both minus signs cancel: $c = \dfrac{q}{m\Delta T} = \dfrac{-3598\ \text{J}}{(60.0\ \text{g})(-66.4\ {}^\circ\text{C})} = $ $0.90\ \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}}$. That is aluminum.

Reactions: from $q$ to $\Delta H$ per mole

Now run a reaction in the cup. The reacting chemicals are the system, and the water is the surroundings. The thermometer sits in the water, so it tells you about the water directly. To talk about the reaction, you have to flip the sign. Since the cup stays open to the air, pressure stays constant, and the heat of the reaction is its enthalpy change, $\Delta H$.

Coffee cup: three lines, always in this order

$$\begin{gathered}q_{\text{water}} = m\,c\,\Delta T \\ q_{\text{rxn}} = -\,q_{\text{water}} \\ \Delta H = \frac{q_{\text{rxn}}}{n}\end{gathered}$$
$m$
mass of the whole solution: water plus whatever you added.
$c$
usually taken as 4.184 J/g·°C, the same as water, for a dilute solution.
$n$
moles of the substance reacted. $\Delta H$ comes out in kJ/mol.

Exam trap: the temperature went up, so $\Delta H$ is negative

This is the most common sign mistake in the unit. A rising thermometer means the water gained heat, so $q_{\text{water}}$ is positive. That heat came from the reaction, so $q_{\text{rxn}}$ and $\Delta H$ are negative. Hot water means exothermic. Cold water means endothermic, and a positive $\Delta H$.

Worked example: $\Delta H$ from a coffee cup

Dissolving 5.00 g of NaOH in 100.0 g of water raises the temperature from 22.0 °C to 34.7 °C. Find $\Delta H$ per mole of NaOH.

  1. Find the total mass and the change: $m = 100.0 + 5.00 = 105.0$ g, and $\Delta T = 34.7 - 22.0 = +12.7\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
  2. Heat gained by the solution: $q_{\text{water}} = (105.0\ \text{g})(4.184\ \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}})(12.7\ {}^\circ\text{C}) = +5580\ \text{J} = +5.58\ \text{kJ}$.
  3. Flip the sign for the reaction: $q_{\text{rxn}} = -5.58\ \text{kJ}$. The water got hotter, so the reaction is exothermic.
  4. Find the moles: $n = 5.00\ \text{g} \div 40.00\ \text{g/mol} = 0.125\ \text{mol}$.
  5. Divide: $\Delta H = \dfrac{-5.58\ \text{kJ}}{0.125\ \text{mol}} = $ $-44.6$ kJ/mol.

The bomb calorimeter

A foam cup would melt if you tried to burn something in it. For combustion, chemists use a bomb calorimeter: a thick steel can filled with oxygen and sunk in water. It is called a bomb because it is sealed tight, and it does not actually blow up.

Bomb calorimeter

$$\begin{gathered}q_{\text{cal}} = C_{\text{cal}}\,\Delta T \\ q_{\text{rxn}} = -\,q_{\text{cal}}\end{gathered}$$
$C_{\text{cal}}$
heat capacity of the whole calorimeter, in kJ/°C. It is found by burning something with a known heat. No mass here: it is for the whole apparatus.
Coffee cupBomb
What stays fixedPressure (open to the air)Volume (sealed steel)
What $q$ measuresEnthalpy change, $\Delta H$Internal energy change, $\Delta E$ (some books write $\Delta U$)
Equation$q = mc\Delta T$$q = C_{\text{cal}}\Delta T$

Worked example: a bomb calorimeter

Burning 1.50 g of glucose ($\ce{C6H12O6}$, 180.16 g/mol) in a bomb with $C_{\text{cal}} = 10.0$ kJ/°C raises the temperature by 2.33 °C. Find the heat of combustion per mole.

  1. Heat gained by the calorimeter: $q_{\text{cal}} = (10.0\ \tfrac{\text{kJ}}{{}^\circ\text{C}})(2.33\ {}^\circ\text{C}) = +23.3\ \text{kJ}$. The °C cancels.
  2. Flip the sign for the reaction: $q_{\text{rxn}} = -23.3\ \text{kJ}$.
  3. Find the moles: $n = 1.50\ \text{g} \div 180.16\ \text{g/mol} = 0.00833\ \text{mol}$.
  4. Divide: $\dfrac{-23.3\ \text{kJ}}{0.00833\ \text{mol}} = $ $-2.80 \times 10^3$ kJ/mol.

Exam trap: two kinds of bomb problems

Some problems give one $C_{\text{cal}}$ for the whole calorimeter. Then $q = C_{\text{cal}}\Delta T$, and you do not use the water's mass. Others give the steel's heat capacity and the mass of water. Then you add the two: $q_{\text{cal}} = C_{\text{bomb}}\Delta T + m_{\text{water}}\,c\,\Delta T$. Read carefully to see which you have.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • In a sealed calorimeter, $q_{\text{hot}} + q_{\text{cold}} = 0$. Both objects end at the same $T_f$.
  • Final temperature: write $m_1c_1(T_f - T_1) + m_2c_2(T_f - T_2) = 0$ and solve for $T_f$.
  • In a reaction, the water is the surroundings: $q_{\text{rxn}} = -q_{\text{water}}$.
  • Water gets hotter means exothermic, $\Delta H < 0$. Water gets colder means endothermic, $\Delta H > 0$.
  • $\Delta H$ per mole: divide $q_{\text{rxn}}$ by the moles reacted.
  • Coffee cup: constant pressure, measures $\Delta H$. Bomb: constant volume, measures $\Delta E$, with $q = C_{\text{cal}}\Delta T$.
