On every exam

Covalent bonds and polarity

Two nonmetals both want electrons, so neither gives any up. They share instead, and the sharing is not always fair.

  • Explain why two atoms bond at a particular distance
  • Read bond length and bond energy from an energy curve
  • Compare single, double and triple bonds
  • Use electronegativity to classify a bond and draw its dipole

Why share?

A hydrogen atom has one electron and would like two. So would its neighbor. Neither one is giving, so they share. Watch what pulls them together, and what stops them from getting too close.

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons, pulled on by two nuclei at once.

The energy well

This is the most important graph in bonding. It shows the energy of two hydrogen atoms at any distance. Lower energy means more stable. Drag the right atom and watch its dot ride the curve. Then let go.

Notice that a real bond never sits perfectly still. It keeps vibrating around the bottom of the well, like a marble in a bowl. I would know: I once dropped a marble into my lab bowl and lost a whole afternoon.

Exam trap: breaking bonds costs energy

To break a bond you have to climb out of the well, so it always takes in energy. Forming a bond releases energy. "Energy stored in the bond" makes students think breaking releases it. It never does.

Worked example: using bond energy

How much energy does it take to break all the H-H bonds in 3.00 g of $\ce{H2}$? The bond energy is 436 kJ/mol.

  1. Find moles of $\ce{H2}$. Its molar mass is $2(1.008) = 2.016$ g/mol: $3.00\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{2.016\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 1.488\text{ mol}$, keeping one extra digit for now.
  2. Each mole of $\ce{H2}$ holds one mole of H-H bonds, so use the bond energy directly.
  3. $1.488\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{436\text{ kJ}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = $ $649$ kJ, to three significant figures.

Single, double, triple

Atoms can share more than one pair. Two shared pairs make a double bond, and three make a triple bond. The number of pairs is the bond order. More shared electrons between the nuclei pull them closer and hold them tighter. Turn up the energy below and see what snaps first.

These bonds to hydrogen show a second trend. Going down the halogens, atoms get bigger, the bond gets longer, and it gets weaker. Values here and in the tester are typical, and they vary a little between sources.

Exam trap: a double bond is not simply twice as strong

C-C is 347 kJ/mol, and C=C is 614 kJ/mol. Two singles would be $2 \times 347 = 694$. The second pair sits in a weaker kind of overlap, as the next lessons will show. The exact ratio depends on the atoms: N-N and O-O have unusually weak single bonds, so theirs come out above 2. Also, the shorter bond is the stronger one, in the same pair of atoms.

A tug of war for electrons

Two identical atoms share perfectly. But different atoms pull with different strength. That pulling strength is electronegativity, and it rises toward fluorine at the top right of the periodic table. Sharing turns unequal, then one-sided.

Classifying a bond by electronegativity difference

$$\Delta\text{EN} = |\text{EN}_1 - \text{EN}_2|$$
Under 0.4
Nonpolar covalent: about equal sharing
0.4 to 1.8
Polar covalent: unequal sharing, partial charges
Over 1.8
Ionic: electrons effectively transferred

Worked example: classify and draw a dipole

Classify the C-O bond and draw its dipole. Electronegativities: C = 2.55, O = 3.44.

  1. Difference: $|3.44 - 2.55| = 0.89$.
  2. This is between 0.4 and 1.8, so it is polar covalent.
  3. O is more electronegative, so O is $\delta-$ and C is $\delta+$.
  4. The dipole arrow points from C to O, with its cross at C: polar covalent, $\overset{\delta+}{\text{C}} - \overset{\delta-}{\text{O}}$.

Exam trap: cutoffs are not sharp lines

Bonds run along a smooth scale from equal sharing to full transfer, and textbooks disagree on the exact cutoffs. Some use 0.5 and 2.0. H-F has a difference of 1.78 and is usually called polar covalent, while metal and nonmetal pairs are usually called ionic regardless. Use your instructor's numbers.

Pocket card

  • Covalent bond: a shared pair pulled on by both nuclei.
  • Bond length: the distance at the energy minimum. Bond energy: the depth of the well. For $\ce{H2}$, 74 pm and 436 kJ/mol.
  • Single to double to triple: shorter and stronger, but the strength does not simply double or triple.
  • Breaking bonds takes in energy. Forming bonds releases it.
  • $\Delta$EN under 0.4 nonpolar, 0.4 to 1.8 polar, over 1.8 ionic. The dipole arrow points to the negative end.
