Often tested

Solubility and colligative properties

Why oil and water will not mix, why soda goes flat, and why salt melts ice. Dissolved particles change more than you would think.

  • Predict what dissolves in what, using like dissolves like
  • Explain how temperature and pressure change solubility, with Henry's law
  • Convert between molality, mole fraction, mass percent, ppm and molarity
  • Calculate freezing point depression, boiling point elevation and osmotic pressure

Like dissolves like

Dissolving is a question of who sticks to whom. A solute dissolves when the new attractions to the solvent can pay for breaking up the old attractions. Polar and ionic things get along with polar solvents. Nonpolar things get along with nonpolar solvents.

Exam trap: oil is not repelled by water

Oil molecules and water molecules do attract each other a little. Oil stays out because water sticks to itself far more strongly. Letting oil in would break hydrogen bonds and offer almost nothing in return. Molecules with both a polar end and a nonpolar end, like ethanol, can get along with both.

Why dissolving gets hot or cold

Dissolving has three energy steps. Two of them cost energy, and one pays energy back. Whichever side wins sets the sign of the enthalpy of solution, $\Delta H_\text{soln}$.

Enthalpy of solution

$$\Delta H_\text{soln} = \Delta H_\text{solute} + \Delta H_\text{solvent} + \Delta H_\text{mix}$$

Temperature and the crystal drop

A saturated solution holds as much as it can at that temperature. An unsaturated one could hold more. For most solids, hot water holds more. Dissolve a lot while hot and cool it very gently, and you can trick it into holding too much: a supersaturated solution. It waits for one small crystal. Try it: heat, add solute, cool, then drop the seed.

Pressure and gases: Henry's law

Pressure hardly matters for solids and liquids, but it matters a lot for gases. Push more gas down onto a liquid and more of it dissolves. A soda bottle is sealed with extra $\ce{CO2}$ pressure. Open it and the pressure drops, so the gas has to come back out.

Henry's law

$$C = k_H \, P$$

Here $C$ is the concentration of dissolved gas, $P$ is the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid, and $k_H$ is Henry's constant, which is different for each gas and temperature. For $\ce{CO2}$ in water at $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$, $k_H$ is about $3.4 \times 10^{-2}$ mol/(L atm).

Worked example: Henry's law

A soda is bottled at $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$ under 2.4 atm of $\ce{CO2}$. What is the concentration of dissolved $\ce{CO2}$? Use $k_H = 3.4 \times 10^{-2}$ mol/(L atm).

  1. Write the law: $C = k_H P$.
  2. Substitute: $C = \left(3.4 \times 10^{-2}\ \dfrac{\text{mol}}{\text{L}\ \cancel{\text{atm}}}\right)(2.4\ \cancel{\text{atm}})$.
  3. Multiply: $C = 0.082$ mol/L, with two significant figures. Doubling the pressure would double this.

Five ways to say how much

Molarity is not the only way to state a concentration. Each unit below is a different fraction. Colligative calculations need molality, because it depends only on masses, so it does not change when the temperature changes the volume.

Molality

$$m = \frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{kilograms of solvent}}$$

Mole fraction

$$\chi_\text{solute} = \frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{total moles}}$$

Mass percent and parts per million

$$\text{mass \%} = \frac{\text{g solute}}{\text{g solution}} \times 100 \qquad \text{ppm} = \frac{\text{g solute}}{\text{g solution}} \times 10^6$$

Exam trap: molality or molarity?

They differ by one letter and they are not the same. Molarity, M, is moles per liter of solution. Molality, m, is moles per kilogram of solvent. Also watch mass percent: the bottom is the mass of the whole solution, solute included.

Colligative properties

Colligative properties depend on how many solute particles there are, not on what they are. Sugar or salt, it makes no difference to the water. It only cares about the count. That leads to four effects: vapor pressure lowering, boiling point elevation, freezing point depression and osmotic pressure.

Colligative properties count particles. They do not care what the particles are, so one mole of NaCl acts like two moles of sugar.

Raoult's law: vapor pressure lowering

$$P_\text{solution} = \chi_\text{solvent}\,P^\circ_\text{solvent}$$

Boiling point elevation and freezing point depression

$$\Delta T_b = i\,K_b\,m \qquad\qquad \Delta T_f = i\,K_f\,m$$

For water, $K_b = 0.512\ ^\circ\text{C}/m$ and $K_f = 1.86\ ^\circ\text{C}/m$. The van 't Hoff factor $i$ counts the particles each formula unit makes: 1 for sugar, 2 for $\ce{NaCl}$, 3 for $\ce{CaCl2}$. Real salts come out a little under these ideal values, because some ions pair up, especially in concentrated solutions.

Worked example: antifreeze

How many grams of ethylene glycol, $\ce{C2H6O2}$ with a molar mass of 62.07 g/mol, must be added to 1.00 kg of water to lower the freezing point to $-10.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$? It does not form ions, so $i = 1$.

  1. Find the change: $\Delta T_f = 0.0 - (-10.0) = 10.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
  2. Rearrange $\Delta T_f = i K_f m$ for molality: $m = \dfrac{\Delta T_f}{i K_f} = \dfrac{10.0\ \cancel{^\circ\text{C}}}{(1)(1.86\ \cancel{^\circ\text{C}}/m)} = 5.38\ m$.
  3. Use the kilograms of solvent: $5.38\ \dfrac{\text{mol}}{\cancel{\text{kg}}} \times 1.00\ \cancel{\text{kg}} = 5.38\text{ mol}$.
  4. Convert to grams: $5.38\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times 62.07\ \dfrac{\text{g}}{\cancel{\text{mol}}} =$ $334\text{ g}$.

Worked example: molar mass from freezing point

Dissolving 5.00 g of an unknown nonelectrolyte in 100.0 g of water lowers the freezing point by $0.930\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Find its molar mass.

  1. Find the molality: $m = \dfrac{\Delta T_f}{i K_f} = \dfrac{0.930}{(1)(1.86)} = 0.500\ m$.
  2. Find the moles, with 100.0 g = 0.1000 kg of solvent: $0.500\ \dfrac{\text{mol}}{\cancel{\text{kg}}} \times 0.1000\ \cancel{\text{kg}} = 0.0500\text{ mol}$.
  3. Molar mass is grams per mole: $\dfrac{5.00\text{ g}}{0.0500\text{ mol}} =$ $100.\text{ g/mol}$.

Salt does not heat the ice. It lowers the temperature at which water can freeze. The ice is still cold, but it can no longer stay solid. Below about $-21\ ^\circ\text{C}$ salt gives up entirely, which is roughly when I give up too.

Osmosis

Separate pure water from a solution using a semipermeable membrane: a wall with holes small enough to let water through but not the solute. Water crosses both ways, but more moves toward the side with more solute. The pressure needed to stop this flow is the osmotic pressure, $\Pi$.

Osmotic pressure

$$\Pi = i\,M\,R\,T$$

Here $M$ is the molarity, $R$ is $0.08206$ L atm/(mol K), and $T$ is in kelvin. Osmotic pressures are surprisingly large, so even small solute amounts can be measured this way. That makes osmosis a great method for finding the molar mass of big molecules such as proteins.

Worked example: saline in the body

What is the osmotic pressure of 0.15 M NaCl at body temperature, $37\ ^\circ\text{C}$?

  1. Find $i$: each NaCl gives $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$, so $i = 2$.
  2. Change to kelvin: $T = 37 + 273 = 310$ K.
  3. Substitute: $\Pi = (2)\left(0.15\ \dfrac{\cancel{\text{mol}}}{\cancel{\text{L}}}\right)\left(0.08206\ \dfrac{\cancel{\text{L}}\text{ atm}}{\cancel{\text{mol}}\ \cancel{\text{K}}}\right)(310\ \cancel{\text{K}})$.
  4. Multiply: $\Pi = 7.6$ atm. This is why IV drips must be matched to your blood, as you will see next.

Exam trap: water moves toward more solute

Students often say water moves "to even out the water" and get the direction backward. Water moves from low solute concentration to high solute concentration. And the solute does not cross the membrane at all. Do not forget $i$ for salts in $\Pi = iMRT$, and use molarity here, not molality.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Like dissolves like: polar and ionic in polar, nonpolar in nonpolar.
  • Solids usually dissolve more when hot. Gases dissolve less when hot, and more under pressure: $C = k_H P$.
  • $m$ = mol solute per kg solvent. $M$ = mol solute per L solution. $\chi$ = moles over total moles.
  • Raoult: $P = \chi_\text{solvent} P^\circ$. Solute lowers vapor pressure.
  • $\Delta T_f = iK_fm$, $\Delta T_b = iK_bm$. For water, $K_f = 1.86$ and $K_b = 0.512\ ^\circ\text{C}/m$. $i$: sugar 1, NaCl 2, $\ce{CaCl2}$ 3.
  • $\Pi = iMRT$. Water moves toward more solute.
