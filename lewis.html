On every exam

Lewis structures

A Lewis structure is a map of where a molecule keeps its outer electrons. One short recipe draws almost all of them.

  • Count the valence electrons in any molecule or ion
  • Follow the recipe to draw a correct Lewis structure
  • Calculate formal charge and use it to pick the best drawing
  • Explain resonance and spot the three octet exceptions

Dots and dashes

Only the outer electrons, called valence electrons, take part in bonding. So a Lewis structure ignores everything else. Each electron is a dot, and each shared pair of electrons is a dash.

Atoms keep sharing until each one sees 8 electrons around it. Hydrogen stops at 2. This is the octet rule.

The recipe

Here is carbon dioxide, drawn with the same steps that work for nearly everything. Watch the electron count in the side panel. It never changes by magic: every electron you place comes out of that pile.

StepWhat to do
1. CountAdd up valence electrons. Add one for each negative charge. Subtract one for each positive charge.
2. SkeletonPut the least electronegative atom in the center. Hydrogen is never in the center.
3. ConnectDraw a single bond to each outer atom. Each bond uses 2 electrons.
4. Outer atomsGive outer atoms lone pairs until each sees 8. Hydrogen stays at 2.
5. CenterPut anything left over on the central atom.
6. ShareIs the center short of 8? Turn a lone pair from an outer atom into a double or triple bond.
7. CheckCount the electrons in your drawing and work out the formal charges.

Worked example: an ion

Draw the Lewis structure of the carbonate ion, $\ce{CO3^{2-}}$.

  1. Count. C brings 4 and each O brings 6: $4 + 3(6) = 22$. The $2-$ charge adds 2 more, so the total is 24 electrons.
  2. Skeleton. Carbon is less electronegative than oxygen, so C goes in the middle with three O around it.
  3. Connect. Three single bonds use $3 \times 2 = 6$ electrons. That leaves $24 - 6 = 18$.
  4. Outer atoms. Each O needs 3 lone pairs, or 6 electrons: $3 \times 6 = 18$. That leaves $18 - 18 = 0$.
  5. Center. Nothing is left, and C only sees 6. So one O shares a lone pair, making a C=O double bond. C now sees 8.
  6. Check. The drawing holds $2(4) + 2(8) = 24$ electrons. Put it in brackets with the charge: one C=O and two C−O bonds, in $[\;]^{2-}$.

Exam trap: the charge changes the count

$\ce{NH4+}$ has $5 + 4(1) - 1 = 8$ electrons, not 9. A positive charge means electrons were removed, and a negative charge means electrons were added. Students flip these all the time.

Your turn

Now you draw. The atoms are already connected with single bonds. Tap an atom to add a lone pair. Tap a bond to make it double, then triple. Rings turn green when an atom is satisfied.

Formal charge

Sometimes several drawings all obey the octet rule. To pick the best one, imagine snipping every bond exactly in half. Each atom keeps its lone electrons and one electron from each bond. Compare that to how many it brought. The difference is its formal charge.

Formal charge

$$\text{FC} = V - L - \tfrac{1}{2}B$$
$V$
Valence electrons the free atom brings
$L$
Electrons in lone pairs on this atom
$B$
Electrons in all the bonds on this atom

Worked example: one structure, every atom

Find the formal charges in $\ce{CO}$, which has a triple bond and one lone pair on each atom.

  1. Carbon: $\text{FC} = 4 - 2 - \tfrac{1}{2}(6) = -1$.
  2. Oxygen: $\text{FC} = 6 - 2 - \tfrac{1}{2}(6) = +1$.
  3. Check: the charges must add up to the charge on the molecule. $-1 + 1 = 0$, which matches neutral CO. C is $-1$ and O is $+1$. Yes, the negative formal charge lands on the less electronegative atom here. It is the only way to give both atoms an octet.
Best structure: the smallest formal charges, with any negative charge on the most electronegative atom.

Resonance

Some molecules have several equally good drawings, and no single drawing tells the truth. This is resonance. Ozone is the classic case.

A mule is not a horse on Mondays and a donkey on Tuesdays. A mule is always a mule, a hybrid of the two. A resonance hybrid is the same. I have been told the mule is less grumpy about it.

Exam trap: resonance is not flipping

The molecule does not switch back and forth between the drawings, and it does not spend time as each one. It has a single structure all the time. Only the electrons in the drawing move, because our notation cannot show a bond and a half. Also, resonance forms never move atoms, only electrons.

When the octet rule breaks

The octet rule is a strong habit, not a law. Exams love the three ways it fails: too few electrons, an odd number of electrons, and too many.

ExceptionWhat happensExamples
Too fewBe is fine with 4, and B and Al are fine with 6$\ce{BeCl2}$, $\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{AlCl3}$
Odd numberAn odd total means one electron has no partner$\ce{NO}$, $\ce{NO2}$
Too manyPeriod 3 and below can hold more than 8$\ce{PCl5}$, $\ce{SF4}$, $\ce{SF6}$, $\ce{XeF4}$, $\ce{I3-}$

Exam trap: period 2 never expands

C, N, O and F can never hold more than 8 electrons. If your drawing gives nitrogen 10, it is wrong. For example, $\ce{NO3-}$ has an octet on N and a $+1$ formal charge, even though two double bonds on N would make that charge vanish.

Textbooks disagree about $\ce{SO4^{2-}}$. Many draw two S=O double bonds so the formal charge on S is 0. Others keep four single bonds and accept the $+2$. The builder shows both. Use the one your instructor uses.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Count valence electrons first. Negative charge adds electrons, positive charge removes them.
  • Center: the least electronegative atom, never H. Bonds first, outer atoms next, leftovers on the center.
  • Center short of 8? Share a lone pair from an outer atom to make a multiple bond.
  • $\text{FC} = V - L - \tfrac{1}{2}B$, and all the formal charges add up to the overall charge.
  • Resonance hybrid: one real structure with identical in-between bonds. It does not flip.
  • Exceptions: B and Be with too few, radicals with an odd count, period 3 and below with too many.
