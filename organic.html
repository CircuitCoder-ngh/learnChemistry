A first look at organic chemistry

One atom with four bonds builds fuels, plastics, medicines and you. Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon.

  • Say why carbon makes four bonds, and what shapes they take
  • Name straight-chain alkanes from methane to octane
  • Spot isomers, and tell alkanes, alkenes and alkynes apart
  • Recognize the common functional groups

Why carbon is special

Most compounds with carbon-hydrogen bonds count as organic. There are millions of them, far more than for any other element. It all comes down to two things carbon does well: it makes four bonds, and it bonds happily to itself.

In stable organic molecules, carbon makes four bonds. Chains give a molecule its skeleton, and functional groups give it its personality.

The real shapes, in 3D

You already met these shapes in VSEPR and hybrid orbitals. Single bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron. A double bond flattens things out, and a triple bond makes a straight line. Turn each molecule, then try to twist it around its carbon-carbon bond.

Exam trap: flat drawings are not flat molecules

We draw methane as a cross because paper is flat. That cross seems to say 90° between bonds, but the real angle is 109.5°. Whenever an exam asks about a shape or an angle, ignore how the drawing looks and count electron groups around the atom.

Climbing the alkane ladder

A hydrocarbon holds only carbon and hydrogen. If every bond is single, it is an alkane, and it holds as many hydrogens as it can, so we call it saturated. The first part of the name counts the carbons, and the ending tells you the family.

For the first four names, try Mice Eat Peanut Butter: methane, ethane, propane, butane. I have tested this advice personally. After that the names follow shapes: pentagon 5, hexagon 6, heptagon 7, octagon 8.

Alkenes and alkynes are unsaturated. Each extra bond between carbons uses up two spots that hydrogens could have filled. These formulas apply to straight, open chains with one multiple bond. Rings also lose two hydrogens each.

Why fuels are hydrocarbons

Natural gas, propane and gasoline are mostly alkanes. Burning one breaks its carbon and hydrogen apart and rebuilds them with oxygen, which is the full subject of combustion equations. The chain builder shows the balanced equation for whichever fuel you make. Longer chains release more heat per mole, because they hold more bonds to burn.

Complete combustion of any alkane

$$\ce{C_{n}H_{2n+2}} + \frac{3n+1}{2}\,\ce{O2} \rightarrow n\,\ce{CO2} + (n+1)\,\ce{H2O}$$

Worked example: burn pentane

Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of pentane.

  1. Pent means 5 carbons, and -ane means all single bonds. Hydrogens: $2(5) + 2 = 12$, so the formula is $\ce{C5H12}$.
  2. Balance carbon first. Five C atoms make $5\ \ce{CO2}$.
  3. Balance hydrogen next. Twelve H atoms make $6\ \ce{H2O}$.
  4. Balance oxygen last. The right side has $5(2) + 6(1) = 16$ O atoms, so you need $8\ \ce{O2}$.
  5. Done: $\ce{C5H12 + 8O2 -> 5CO2 + 6H2O}$. Each side has 5 C, 12 H and 16 O.

Same atoms, different molecule

Atoms can connect in more than one way. Molecules with the same formula but different connections are isomers. They are truly different substances, with different boiling points and sometimes wildly different behavior.

Exam trap: a bent drawing is not an isomer

$\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH3}$ drawn with a kink in it is still butane. Trace the connections: if you can follow one unbroken path through all four carbons, it is the same molecule. Isomers need different connections, not different poses.

To name a branched alkane, find the longest chain, even if it bends in the drawing. Number from the end nearer the branch. Name the branch by its size: one carbon is methyl, and two carbons is ethyl.

Worked example: name a branched alkane

Name $\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3}$.

  1. Find the longest chain, skipping the $\ce{CH3}$ in parentheses: $\ce{CH3-CH-CH2-CH3}$ is 4 carbons, so the parent is butane.
  2. Number from the end nearer the branch. The branch sits on carbon 2 from the left, and on carbon 3 from the right, so count from the left.
  3. The branch $\ce{CH3}$ has one carbon, so it is methyl.
  4. Put it together: 2-methylbutane. Check: 5 carbons and 12 hydrogens, so it is an isomer of pentane.

Functional groups

A functional group is a small cluster of atoms that sets how a molecule behaves. Think of the carbon chain as a plain phone, and the group as the case and apps that make it yours. A molecule with the same group tends to act the same way, however long its chain is.

An easy way to tell the carbonyl groups apart: a C=O at the end of a chain, with an H on it, is an aldehyde. In the middle, it is a ketone. With an extra OH on that same carbon, it is a carboxylic acid.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Carbon makes 4 bonds. Single bonds point to tetrahedron corners, at 109.5°. Double bonds give about 120°, and triple bonds give 180°.
  • Prefixes: meth, eth, prop, but, pent, hex, hept, oct for 1 to 8 carbons.
  • Alkane $\ce{C_{n}H_{2n+2}}$, alkene $\ce{C_{n}H_{2n}}$, alkyne $\ce{C_{n}H_{2n-2}}$. Only alkanes are saturated.
  • Isomers have the same formula and different connections. A bent drawing is not an isomer.
  • Groups to know: alcohol $\ce{-OH}$, ether $\ce{C-O-C}$, aldehyde $\ce{-CHO}$, ketone $\ce{C=O}$ mid-chain, acid $\ce{-COOH}$, ester $\ce{-COO-}$, amine $\ce{-NH2}$.
  • Burning any hydrocarbon completely gives $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$. Balance C, then H, then O.
