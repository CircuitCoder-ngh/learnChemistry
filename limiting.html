On every exam

Limiting reactants and yield

A reaction stops when one ingredient runs out. Find which one, and you know exactly how much you can make.

  • Find the limiting reactant by comparing moles with the recipe, never by grams
  • Work out how much product forms and how much excess is left over
  • Use two different methods and get the same answer
  • Calculate theoretical yield and percent yield

The sandwich shop runs out

Real reactions rarely get ingredients in the perfect amounts. There is nearly always too much of something. So the big question is: what runs out first?

The limiting reactant is the one that runs out first. It alone decides how much product you get.

The other one is the excess reactant. It is not useless. It reacts right up until the limiting one is gone, and then it just stands around, like me after the sandwiches run out.

The mass surprise

Here is the mistake that costs the most marks. Students see two masses and call the smaller one limiting. It sounds so reasonable. Watch it fail.

Exam trap: smallest mass is not limiting

Grams tell you how heavy, not how many. Reactions run by counting. An exam will happily give you 20 g of one reactant and 10 g of another, with the 20 g sample running out first. Always change to moles, then compare against the coefficients.

Now try it with your own numbers. The calculation pipeline runs from grams to moles to recipe runs. Find a mix where the sample with more grams is the limiting one, or press the button if you want to see one straight away.

Two methods, same answer

Your instructor may teach either one, so learn both. They always agree, and either one starts by changing every mass to moles. Follow one problem all the way through.

Method 1: recipe runs

$$\text{recipe runs} = \frac{\text{moles you have}}{\text{coefficient}}$$
Limiting
the reactant with the fewest recipe runs

Method 2: product from each

$$\text{product} = \text{moles of reactant} \times \frac{\text{mol product}}{\text{mol reactant}}$$
Limiting
the reactant that makes the least product. That smallest amount is the real answer.

Worked example: method 1 with leftovers

15.0 g of $\ce{N2}$ reacts with 5.00 g of $\ce{H2}$ by $\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}$. Which is limiting? How much $\ce{NH3}$ forms? How much excess is left?

  1. Change both to moles. $\ce{N2}$: $15.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{28.01\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 0.5355\text{ mol}$. $\ce{H2}$: $5.00\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{2.016\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 2.480\text{ mol}$.
  2. Divide each by its coefficient. $\ce{N2}$: $0.5355 \div 1 = 0.5355$ runs. $\ce{H2}$: $2.480 \div 3 = 0.8267$ runs.
  3. $\ce{N2}$ allows fewer runs, so $\ce{N2}$ is limiting. Notice that it also has the most grams.
  4. Use the limiting reactant for everything else. $0.5355\ \cancel{\text{mol N}_2} \times \dfrac{2\text{ mol NH}_3}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol N}_2}} = 1.071\text{ mol NH}_3$. Then $1.071\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{17.03\text{ g}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = $ $18.2\text{ g NH}_3$.
  5. Find the $\ce{H2}$ used. $0.5355\ \cancel{\text{mol N}_2} \times \dfrac{3\text{ mol H}_2}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol N}_2}} = 1.607\text{ mol H}_2$, which is $1.607 \times 2.016 = 3.24\text{ g}$.
  6. Subtract. $5.00\text{ g} - 3.24\text{ g} = $ $1.76\text{ g H}_2$ left over. Check the mass: $15.0 + 5.00 = 20.0$ g in, and $18.2 + 1.76 = 20.0$ g out.

A tidy way to organize leftovers is a table of start, change and end. Some courses call it a BCA table (before, change, after). It is the same table the reactor builds, and it has the same shape as the ICE table (initial, change, equilibrium) you will meet in equilibrium. Worked example: method 2

12.0 g of Mg burns with 10.0 g of $\ce{O2}$ by $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$. How many grams of $\ce{MgO}$ can form?

  1. Pretend Mg runs out. $12.0\ \cancel{\text{g Mg}} \times \dfrac{1\ \cancel{\text{mol Mg}}}{24.31\ \cancel{\text{g Mg}}} \times \dfrac{2\ \cancel{\text{mol MgO}}}{2\ \cancel{\text{mol Mg}}} \times \dfrac{40.30\text{ g MgO}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol MgO}}} = 19.9\text{ g MgO}$.
  2. Pretend $\ce{O2}$ runs out. $10.0\ \cancel{\text{g O}_2} \times \dfrac{1\ \cancel{\text{mol O}_2}}{32.00\ \cancel{\text{g O}_2}} \times \dfrac{2\ \cancel{\text{mol MgO}}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol O}_2}} \times \dfrac{40.30\text{ g MgO}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol MgO}}} = 25.2\text{ g MgO}$.
  3. The smaller number is the real one. Mg stops the reaction at $19.9\text{ g MgO}$, so Mg is limiting and $\ce{O2}$ is in excess.

With method 2, never add the two product amounts together. Only one of them really happens. The other is a daydream about a world where the first reactant never ran out.

Theoretical and percent yield

The map gives you the most product the atoms allow. That number is the theoretical yield. A real experiment nearly always gives less. What you really collect is the actual yield.

Percent yield

$$\text{percent yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\%$$
Actual
what you measured in the lab. A problem has to tell you this.
Theoretical
what you calculate from the limiting reactant

Worked example: percent yield from the start

20.0 g of $\ce{CaCO3}$ is heated and breaks down by $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$. You collect 9.80 g of $\ce{CaO}$. What is the percent yield?

  1. There is only one reactant, so it is limiting. Change it to moles. $20.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{100.09\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 0.1998\text{ mol CaCO}_3$.
  2. Use the mole ratio. $0.1998\ \cancel{\text{mol CaCO}_3} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol CaO}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol CaCO}_3}} = 0.1998\text{ mol CaO}$.
  3. Find the theoretical yield. $0.1998\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{56.08\text{ g}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = 11.2\text{ g CaO}$.
  4. Divide what you got by what was possible. $\dfrac{9.80\ \cancel{\text{g}}}{11.2\ \cancel{\text{g}}} \times 100\% = $ $87.5\%$. A percent has no other unit, since the grams cancel.

Exam trap: theoretical yield comes from the limiting reactant

Compute it from the excess reactant and you get an answer that is too big, and it even looks reasonable. Also check your fraction: actual goes on top. A yield over 100% does not mean you made atoms. It usually means the product is still wet or impure.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • The limiting reactant runs out first and sets how much product forms. The excess reactant has some left over.
  • Never compare grams. Change to moles, then divide each by its coefficient. The smallest result is limiting.
  • Method 2: find the product from each reactant. The smaller amount is the real answer.
  • Excess left = what you started with minus what the limiting reactant used up.
  • Percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100. Use the limiting reactant to get the theoretical yield.
