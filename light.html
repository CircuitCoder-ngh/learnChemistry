Often tested

Light and photons

Light acts like a wave and like a stream of tiny packets. You will need both ideas to understand atoms.

  • Name the parts of a wave and use $c = \lambda\nu$
  • Place any kind of light on the spectrum and order it by energy
  • Find the energy of one photon, and of a mole of photons
  • Explain why the photoelectric effect proves light comes in packets

Light is a wave

Moe's lab is underground, so light matters down here. Before we ask what light does to atoms, we need words for how a wave looks. Step through the story.

Speed of light

$$c = \lambda \nu$$
$c$
speed of light, $2.998 \times 10^{8}$ m/s
$\lambda$
wavelength, in meters (the Greek letter lambda)
$\nu$
frequency, in hertz, where 1 Hz = 1 per second (the Greek letter nu)

Worked example: wavelength to frequency

Hydrogen gas glows with a red line at 656 nm. What is the frequency of this light?

  1. Convert nanometers to meters, so the units match $c$: $656\ \cancel{\text{nm}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ m}}{10^{9}\ \cancel{\text{nm}}} = 6.56 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}$.
  2. Rearrange $c = \lambda\nu$ to get frequency alone: $\nu = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$.
  3. Put the numbers in: $\nu = \dfrac{2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \cancel{\text{m}}/\text{s}}{6.56 \times 10^{-7}\ \cancel{\text{m}}}$.
  4. The answer is $4.57 \times 10^{14}$ Hz. Meters cancel, leaving 1/s, which is Hz.

Exam trap: forgetting to convert nanometers

$c$ is in meters per second, so $\lambda$ must be in meters. Plugging in 656 instead of $6.56 \times 10^{-7}$ gives a frequency a billion times too small. Remember: $1\text{ nm} = 10^{-9}\text{ m}$.

The spectrum: far more than rainbows

Visible light is a tiny slice of a huge family called the electromagnetic spectrum. All of it moves at the same speed $c$. Only the wavelength changes, from smaller than an atom to longer than a building.

A memory trick for the order, from long wavelength to short: Radio, Microwave, Infrared, Visible, Ultraviolet, X-ray, Gamma. Red has the lowest energy of the colors you can see. Violet has the highest.

Light comes in packets

Around 1900, the wave idea started to fail. Max Planck found that energy is not smooth. It comes in tiny fixed amounts. Each packet of light is called a photon.

One photon carries energy $E = h\nu$. Higher frequency means more energy in each packet. A brighter lamp sends more packets, not stronger ones.

Energy of one photon

$$E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$
$E$
energy of one photon, in joules
$h$
Planck's constant, $6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s

Worked example: one photon, then a mole of them

Find the energy of one 656 nm photon, and of a mole of them in kJ/mol.

  1. Use the frequency form. From before, $\nu = 4.57 \times 10^{14}\text{ Hz}$, and 1 Hz is 1/s.
  2. $E = h\nu = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\cancel{\text{s}})(4.57 \times 10^{14}\ \cancel{\text{s}^{-1}}) = 3.03 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$ per photon.
  3. A mole holds $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ photons: $\dfrac{3.03 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}}{\cancel{\text{photon}}} \times \dfrac{6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \cancel{\text{photons}}}{1\text{ mol}} = 1.82 \times 10^{5}\text{ J/mol}$.
  4. Divide by 1000 to get kilojoules: $182$ kJ/mol. That is the same size as the energy in chemical bonds.

Exam trap: per photon or per mole?

$E = hc/\lambda$ gives joules for one photon. Bond energies are given in kJ for a mole of bonds. Before you compare them, multiply by Avogadro's number and divide by 1000.

The photoelectric effect

Shine light on a clean metal and electrons can pop out. Nothing strange so far. The strange part is which light works. Watch the experiment whose explanation won Einstein his Nobel Prize.

Photoelectric equation

$$KE = h\nu - \phi$$
$$\nu_0 = \frac{\phi}{h}$$
$KE$
kinetic energy of one freed electron
$\phi$
work function: the energy needed to free one electron from this metal (the Greek letter phi)
$\nu_0$
threshold frequency: below this, no electrons come out, however bright the light

Exam trap: brighter is not stronger

Below the threshold frequency, a brighter lamp does nothing. Above it, a brighter lamp frees more electrons, and so makes more current, but each electron has the same kinetic energy. Only frequency changes the kinetic energy.

Worked example: how fast does the electron leave?

Sodium has a work function of $3.68 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Violet light at 400 nm hits it. What is the kinetic energy of each electron?

  1. Multiply the constants: $hc = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\cancel{\text{s}})(2.998 \times 10^{8}\text{ m}/\cancel{\text{s}}) = 1.986 \times 10^{-25}\text{ J}\cdot\text{m}$.
  2. Find the photon energy: $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{1.986 \times 10^{-25}\text{ J}\cdot\cancel{\text{m}}}{4.00 \times 10^{-7}\ \cancel{\text{m}}} = 4.97 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$.
  3. Compare. $4.97 \times 10^{-19}$ J is more than $3.68 \times 10^{-19}$ J, so electrons do come out.
  4. Whatever is left over becomes motion: $KE = E - \phi = 4.97 \times 10^{-19} - 3.68 \times 10^{-19}$.
  5. The answer is $1.29 \times 10^{-19}$ J per electron.

So is light a wave or a particle? It is both, and neither. It spreads like a wave and lands like a particle. Chemists call this wave-particle duality. I call it a strange way to run a universe.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • $c = \lambda\nu$, with $c = 2.998 \times 10^{8}$ m/s. Long wavelength means low frequency.
  • $E = h\nu = hc/\lambda$, with $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s. High frequency means high energy.
  • Long to short wavelength: radio, microwave, infrared, visible (red to violet), ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma.
  • Convert: $1\text{ nm} = 10^{-9}\text{ m}$. Per mole: multiply by $6.022 \times 10^{23}$.
  • Photoelectric: $KE = h\nu - \phi$. Below threshold, no electrons at any brightness.
  • Brightness means number of photons. Color means energy per photon.
