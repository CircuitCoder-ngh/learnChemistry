Often tested

What is stuff made of?

All stuff is particles. What they are, and what they are doing, explains almost everything you can see.

  • Describe solids, liquids and gases at the particle level
  • Classify any sample as an element, a compound or a mixture
  • Prove whether a change is physical or chemical
  • Sort properties as physical or chemical, intensive or extensive

Same molecules, three states

Ice, water and steam look like three different things. They are the same molecules, doing three different things. Slide the temperature and watch. These molecules really do attract each other in this simulation, so the states appear on their own.

Heating does not change the particles. It changes how fast they move and how far apart they get.

There is a fourth state. Heat a gas to thousands of degrees and electrons rip off the atoms, giving a plasma. Lightning and the Sun are plasma. Please do not try it in my kitchen.

Sorting out matter

Every sample of matter fits into one of four boxes, and you decide by asking what the particles look like. Step through them, then try the game.

Pure substance: one kind of building block, always in the same ratio. In salt, that block is a matched pair of ions. Mixture: more than one kind, not bonded together.

Exam trap: two atoms joined does not mean compound

$\ce{O2}$ is an element, even though its atoms come in pairs, because both atoms are oxygen. A compound needs two or more different elements bonded together. A mix of $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{N2}$ is a mixture of two elements, not a compound.

Physical or chemical change?

Here is the one question that decides it: are the particles the same afterward? If the same molecules are there, only moved around, it is a physical change. If atoms have changed partners and new substances exist, it is a chemical change.

Exam trap: the bubbles in boiling water

The bubbles are water vapor, $\ce{H2O(g)}$. They are not hydrogen and oxygen gas. Boiling only pulls whole molecules away from each other. The covalent bonds inside each molecule never break. By the way, you cannot see water vapor. The white cloud above a kettle is tiny droplets of liquid water.

Exam trap: dissolving is not a chemical change

Sugar seems to vanish in tea, but every sugar molecule is still there, whole. Salt splits into its ions, and yet you still get the same salt back by letting the water evaporate. Courses count both as physical changes.

Worked example: use the particle test

A silver spoon turns black in air. Physical or chemical?

  1. Ask what the black stuff is. It is silver sulfide, $\ce{Ag2S}$, and it is not silver.
  2. Ask whether the atoms changed partners. Silver atoms were bonded only to silver. Now they are bonded to sulfur.
  3. A new substance formed, so this is a chemical change. The color change was a clue, and the particles are the proof.

Properties: two ways to sort them

A physical property can be measured without changing what the substance is: color, density, melting point. A chemical property describes how a substance can turn into something else: it burns, it rusts, it reacts with acid.

There is a second sorting. An extensive property depends on how much you have. An intensive property does not. Cut this bar and see which is which.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Solid: close, in place, vibrating. Liquid: close, sliding. Gas: far apart, flying.
  • Element: one kind of atom, even in pairs like $\ce{O2}$. Compound: different atoms bonded in a fixed ratio.
  • Homogeneous mixture: the same everywhere. Heterogeneous mixture: visibly different regions.
  • Physical change: same particles. Chemical change: atoms change partners, and new substances form.
  • Boiling and dissolving are physical. Bubbles in boiling water are $\ce{H2O(g)}$.
  • Extensive depends on amount (mass, volume). Intensive does not (density, melting point).
