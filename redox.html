On every exam

Oxidation and reduction

Some reactions are electrons changing owners. A short set of number rules lets you follow them through any reaction, from rust to a burning flame.

  • Say which species is oxidized and which is reduced
  • Assign oxidation numbers using the rules in order
  • Name the oxidizing agent and the reducing agent
  • Predict reactions with the activity series, and balance electrons

Electrons change owners

Put a strip of zinc into blue copper sulfate solution. Within minutes the strip grows a rusty brown coat and the blue begins to fade. The picture below shows why.

Oxidation is losing electrons. Reduction is gaining electrons. One never happens without the other.

Two ways to remember it. OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain. Or LEO says GER: Lose Electrons Oxidation, Gain Electrons Reduction. I prefer the lion. I am told that moles should not, for safety reasons.

Half-reactions show each side of the trade

$$\text{Oxidation: }\ce{Zn -> Zn^2+ + 2e-}$$ $$\text{Reduction: }\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}$$ $$\text{Overall: }\ce{Zn + Cu^2+ -> Zn^2+ + Cu}$$

Exam trap: the agent is named for what it does to the other one

The oxidizing agent causes oxidation, so it takes the electrons, and it gets reduced. The reducing agent causes reduction, so it gives the electrons, and it gets oxidized. In this reaction, $\ce{Cu^2+}$ is the oxidizing agent and $\ce{Zn}$ is the reducing agent.

Oxidation numbers: tracking the electrons

Sometimes you cannot see electrons move, because the atoms share them. So chemists use a bookkeeping number called the oxidation number. It says how many electrons an atom has lost or gained, assuming the more electronegative partner takes them all.

If an atom's number goes up, it was oxidized. If it goes down, it was reduced. Use these rules, and when two rules disagree, the higher one wins.

PriorityRuleExample
1A free element is 0$\ce{Fe}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{S8}$ are all 0
2A one-atom ion equals its charge$\ce{Na+}$ is +1, $\ce{S^2-}$ is −2
3Group 1 is +1. Group 2 is +2. Aluminum is +3Na in $\ce{NaCl}$ is +1
4Fluorine is −1F in $\ce{OF2}$ is −1
5Hydrogen is +1, but −1 with a metalH in $\ce{H2O}$ is +1, in $\ce{NaH}$ is −1
6Oxygen is −2, but −1 in peroxidesO in $\ce{CO2}$ is −2, in $\ce{H2O2}$ is −1
7Cl, Br, I are −1, unless with O or a lighter halogenCl in $\ce{HCl}$ is −1, in $\ce{ClO4-}$ is +7
8All the numbers add up to the total charge$\ce{SO4^2-}$: S + 4(−2) = −2

Worked example: a plain polyatomic ion

What is the oxidation number of Cr in $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$?

  1. Rule 6: each oxygen is −2.
  2. Rule 8: the numbers must add up to the charge of the ion, which is −2.
  3. Write it with $x$ for chromium: $2x + 7(-2) = -2$.
  4. Simplify: $2x - 14 = -2$, so $2x = 12$.
  5. So $x = +6$.

Worked example: spot the change

In $\ce{2Fe^2+ + Cl2 -> 2Fe^3+ + 2Cl-}$, what is oxidized and what is reduced?

  1. Iron goes from +2 to +3. The number went up, so $\ce{Fe^2+}$ lost an electron and was oxidized.
  2. Chlorine goes from 0 in $\ce{Cl2}$ to −1 in $\ce{Cl-}$. The number went down, so $\ce{Cl2}$ gained electrons and was reduced.
  3. $\ce{Cl2}$ took electrons, so it is the oxidizing agent. $\ce{Fe^2+}$ gave them, so it is the reducing agent.
  4. Check the electrons: 2 Fe each lose 1, and 2 Cl each gain 1. 2 electrons lost, 2 electrons gained.

Exam trap: sign first, and the number can exceed an ion's charge

Write oxidation numbers with the sign in front, such as +6, to tell them apart from ionic charges, which go 6+. And $\ce{S}$ in $\ce{SO4^2-}$ being +6 does not mean a real $\ce{S^6+}$ ion exists. It is bookkeeping, not a measurement.

The activity series: who wins the electrons?

Metals differ in how readily they give up electrons. Rank them and you have the activity series. A metal gives electrons to the ion of any metal below it on the ladder. It cannot take over from a metal above it.

So zinc hands its electrons to copper ions, and copper metal forms. Put copper in zinc sulfate and nothing happens at all. Hydrogen is on the ladder too, so you can tell which metals dissolve in acid.

Redox is everywhere

Rust, fire, batteries, and bleach are all redox. And nothing has to look ionic. Oxidation numbers follow the electrons, even in a flame.

Here is the half-reaction method for a harder case, a reaction in acid. Exams at this level often ask for this one. Balance each half on its own, then match the electrons.

Worked example: half-reactions in acid

Balance $\ce{MnO4-}$ + $\ce{Fe^2+}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{Mn^2+}$ + $\ce{Fe^3+}$ in acid.

  1. Oxidation half: $\ce{Fe^2+ -> Fe^3+ + e-}$.
  2. Reduction half, atoms other than O and H first: $\ce{MnO4- -> Mn^2+}$.
  3. Add $\ce{H2O}$ to balance O: $\ce{MnO4- -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O}$.
  4. Add $\ce{H+}$ to balance H: $\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O}$.
  5. Add electrons to balance charge. The left is +7 and the right is +2, so add 5 to the left: $\ce{MnO4-}$ + $\ce{8H+}$ + $\ce{5e-}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{Mn^2+}$ + $\ce{4H2O}$.
  6. Match electrons: multiply the iron half by 5, then add them up. The electrons cancel.
  7. Result: $\ce{MnO4-}$ + $\ce{8H+}$ + $\ce{5Fe^2+}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{Mn^2+}$ + $\ce{4H2O}$ + $\ce{5Fe^3+}$. Check the charge: $-1 + 8 + 10 = +17$ on the left, and $2 + 15 = +17$ on the right.

Pocket card

  • OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss of electrons, Reduction Is Gain.
  • If an oxidation number goes up, it was oxidized. If it goes down, it was reduced.
  • The oxidizing agent takes electrons and is reduced. The reducing agent gives electrons and is oxidized.
  • Oxidation numbers always add up to the total charge.
  • A metal displaces the ion of any metal below it in the activity series.
  • Electrons lost must equal electrons gained. Multiply the half-reactions until they match.
