Three questions: Is it an acid? Is there a metal? Can that metal have more than one charge?

Naming ionic compounds

Ionic names are the easiest: say the cation, then the anion. The only catch is the metals that can have more than one charge.

Worked example: formula to name

Name $\ce{Mn(NO3)2}$.

  1. Follow the flowchart. It does not start with H, and manganese is a metal, so it is ionic.
  2. Manganese has several possible charges, so it needs a Roman numeral.
  3. Nitrate is $\ce{NO3-}$. Two of them total $2 \times (-1) = -2$.
  4. The compound is neutral, so the one manganese ion must be $+2$.
  5. The name is manganese(II) nitrate.

Worked example: name to formula

Write the formula of iron(III) sulfate.

  1. The Roman numeral gives the charge directly: iron(III) is $\ce{Fe^3+}$. Sulfate is $\ce{SO4^2-}$.
  2. Balance the charges. The lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6.
  3. $2 \times (+3) = +6$ and $3 \times (-2) = -6$, so use 2 irons and 3 sulfates.
  4. More than one polyatomic ion needs parentheses: $\ce{Fe2(SO4)3}$.

Exam trap: the Roman numeral is a charge, not a count

$\ce{Fe2O3}$ is not iron(II) oxide just because it has 2 irons. Each iron is $+3$, so it is iron(III) oxide. And in copper(II) oxide, $\ce{CuO}$, there is only one copper. Always calculate the charge from the anion. Older books may say ferrous and ferric for iron(II) and iron(III), or cuprous and cupric for copper(I) and copper(II).

Naming molecular compounds

Nonmetals have no fixed charges to do the math for you, so the name has to count the atoms. That is what prefixes are for.

Exam trap: do not mix up the two systems

Prefixes are only for molecular compounds. $\ce{MgCl2}$ is not magnesium dichloride. Roman numerals are only for ionic compounds. $\ce{CO2}$ is not carbon(IV) oxide on your exam. And molecular formulas do not simplify, so $\ce{N2O4}$ is dinitrogen tetroxide, not nitrogen dioxide.

Naming acids

Acids are hydrogen plus an anion, dissolved in water. So the name depends entirely on the anion's ending. You just need to know one little code.

Exam trap: sulfuric or hydrosulfuric?

$\ce{H2SO4}$ is sulfuric acid, from sulfate. $\ce{H2S}$ is hydrosulfuric acid, from sulfide. The hydro- prefix only appears when the anion has no oxygen. Also, $\ce{HCl}$ as a dry gas is hydrogen chloride. It only becomes hydrochloric acid when dissolved in water, written $\ce{HCl(aq)}$.

Hydrates: crystals with water inside

Some salts lock water molecules into their crystals. The water is part of the formula, written after a dot. Name the salt, then add a prefix and the word hydrate. Heat one and watch the water leave.

Pocket card

  • Acid? Use the anion: -ide becomes hydro-...-ic, -ate becomes -ic, -ite becomes -ous.
  • Metal or ammonium? Ionic: say the cation, then the anion. No prefixes.
  • Several charges possible? Add a Roman numeral equal to the metal's charge.
  • Only nonmetals? Molecular: use prefixes, skip mono- on the first element, end in -ide.
  • Prefixes: mono, di, tri, tetra, penta, hexa, hepta, octa, nona, deca.
  • Hydrates: salt name + prefix + hydrate. $\ce{CuSO4*5H2O}$ is copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate.
