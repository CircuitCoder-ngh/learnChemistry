pH and buffers

pH is a way of counting zeros. It turns impossibly tiny numbers into a friendly scale, and buffers are what hold it steady.

  • Say what one step on the pH scale really means

  • Tell strong from weak acids, and use $K_a$
  • Explain a buffer and use Henderson-Hasselbalch

Counting zeros

You know from the acids and bases lesson that acids hand over protons. In water, that proton lands on a water molecule and makes $\ce{H3O+}$, the hydronium ion. Many books write it as $\ce{H+}$ for short, and they mean the same thing.

Each step down the pH scale means 10 times more $\ce{H3O+}$. Lemon juice at pH 2 has 100,000 times more than pure water at pH 7.

The four numbers, all at 25 °C

$$\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H3O+}] \qquad [\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-\text{pH}}$$ $$\text{pOH} = -\log[\ce{OH-}] \qquad \text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$$ $$K_w = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$$

Worked example: from concentration to pH

Black coffee and rain are around pH 5 to 6. A soda has $[\ce{H3O+}] = 3.2 \times 10^{-4}$ M. Find its pH, pOH and $[\ce{OH-}]$.

  1. Estimate first. $10^{-4}$ would be pH 4 and $10^{-3}$ would be pH 3, so the answer sits between 3 and 4.
  2. Calculate: $\text{pH} = -\log(3.2 \times 10^{-4}) = $ $3.49$.
  3. Use the sum: $\text{pOH} = 14.00 - 3.49 = $ $10.51$.
  4. Use $K_w$: $[\ce{OH-}] = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{3.2 \times 10^{-4}} = $ $3.1 \times 10^{-11}$ M.

Exam trap: sig figs in a log

Only the digits after the decimal point in a pH count as significant. The number in front just holds the power of ten. So $3.2 \times 10^{-4}$, with two sig figs, gives a pH with two decimal places: 3.49, not 3.5.

Strong and weak acids

Two acids can have the same concentration and very different pH. The difference is what share of their molecules actually hand over a proton.

Exam trap: strong does not mean concentrated

Strong and weak describe the share of molecules that ionize. Concentrated and dilute describe how much acid is in the water. A dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid are both real things. Weak also does not mean safe: HF is a weak acid and is very dangerous.

Weak acids and bases

$$K_a = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]} \qquad K_a K_b = K_w$$ $$\text{percent ionized} = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}]}{[\ce{HA}]_0} \times 100$$

Worked example: pH of a weak acid

Find the pH of 0.10 M acetic acid, with $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$.

  1. Set up an ICE table. $$\ce{CH3COOH + H2O <=> H3O+ + CH3COO-}$$ The bottom row is $[\ce{HA}] = 0.10 - x$, and $[\ce{H3O+}] = [\ce{A-}] = x$.
  2. Put it into $K_a$: $1.8 \times 10^{-5} = \dfrac{x^2}{0.10 - x}$.
  3. Since $K_a$ is so small, $x$ is tiny next to 0.10. Treat $0.10 - x$ as just $0.10$: $x^2 = (1.8 \times 10^{-5})(0.10) = 1.8 \times 10^{-6}$.
  4. Take the root: $x = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-6}} = 1.34 \times 10^{-3}$ M.
  5. Check the shortcut. $\dfrac{1.34 \times 10^{-3}}{0.10} \times 100 = 1.3\%$, which is under the usual 5% limit, so it was fine.
  6. $\text{pH} = -\log(1.34 \times 10^{-3}) = $ $2.87$. A strong acid at the same concentration would be pH 1.00.

Buffers: holding pH steady

Your blood stays between pH 7.35 and 7.45, whatever you eat. A buffer makes that possible. It has one partner ready to catch acid and another ready to catch base.

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation

$$\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}$$
$\text{p}K_a$
$-\log K_a$. When $[\ce{A-}] = [\ce{HA}]$, the log term is zero and $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$.

Worked example: a buffer takes a hit

One liter of buffer holds 0.10 mol of acetic acid and 0.10 mol of acetate, with $\text{p}K_a = 4.74$. You add 0.010 mol of $\ce{HCl}$. Find the new pH.

  1. Do the reaction first. The strong acid reacts completely with the base partner. $$\ce{H3O+ + CH3COO- -> CH3COOH + H2O}$$
  2. Update the moles. Acetate falls: $0.10 - 0.010 = 0.09$ mol. Acetic acid rises: $0.10 + 0.010 = 0.11$ mol.
  3. Use Henderson-Hasselbalch: $\text{pH} = 4.74 + \log\dfrac{0.09}{0.11} = 4.74 - 0.09 = $ $4.65$.
  4. Compare: the same acid in a liter of plain water takes the pH from 7.00 all the way to 2.00.

A buffer is not magic. It is a weak acid and its partner, standing by with a mop and a bucket. Give it more mess than it has mops, and pH will crash just the same. Try it above.

Exam trap: what makes a buffer

A buffer needs a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid). $\ce{HCl}$ with $\ce{NaCl}$ is no buffer, because $\ce{Cl-}$ is too weak a base to catch any protons.

Pocket card

  • $\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H3O+}]$. One step is a factor of 10. Lower pH is more acidic.
  • At 25 °C, $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$, and $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$.
  • Strong acids ionize completely: pH comes straight from molarity. Weak acids need $K_a$ and an ICE table.
  • A larger $K_a$ means a stronger acid. $K_a K_b = K_w$ for a conjugate pair.
  • A buffer is a weak acid plus its conjugate base. Use $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}$.
  • Strong acid or base added to a buffer: do the reaction with moles first, then Henderson-Hasselbalch.
