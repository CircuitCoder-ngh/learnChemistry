Periodic trends

Size, grip on electrons, and hunger for more all change in steady patterns across the table. One tug of war explains nearly all of it.

  • Use effective nuclear charge and shielding to explain any trend
  • Predict how radius, ionization energy, electron affinity and electronegativity change
  • Compare the sizes of atoms and ions, including isoelectronic series
  • Explain the Be to B and N to O dips, and read successive ionization energies

One tug of war explains it all

Every outer electron is in a tug of war. The nucleus pulls it in. The inner electrons get in the way. Once you see that, you can stop memorizing trends and start predicting them.

Effective nuclear charge, the simple estimate

$$Z_{\text{eff}} \approx Z - \text{core electrons}$$
$Z$
the number of protons
$Z_{\text{eff}}$
the pull an outer electron actually feels. More careful methods, such as Slater's rules, give somewhat different numbers, but the same trends.

Worked example: estimating $Z_{\text{eff}}$

Estimate the effective nuclear charge felt by the valence electrons of sulfur. Then compare with oxygen, in the same group.

  1. Sulfur has $Z = 16$ and the configuration $\mathrm{[Ne]\,3s^{2}\,3p^{4}}$, so it has 10 core electrons.
  2. $Z_{\text{eff}} \approx 16 - 10 = +6$.
  3. Oxygen has $Z = 8$ and the configuration $\mathrm{[He]\,2s^{2}\,2p^{4}}$, so it has 2 core electrons: $Z_{\text{eff}} \approx 8 - 2 = +6$.
  4. The same pull, but oxygen's outer electrons are in $n = 2$, much closer. So oxygen is smaller and holds its electrons more tightly.

Across a period, the pull grows at about the same distance. Down a group, the pull stays about the same but the distance grows. Atoms get small and grabby toward the top right, and big and giving toward the bottom left.

Exam trap: more electrons does not mean bigger

Going from sodium to chlorine adds six electrons, yet chlorine is much smaller. Those electrons all go in the same shell, while protons are added too. So $Z_{\text{eff}}$ rises and the whole shell is pulled inward.

Electronegativity is how hard an atom pulls on shared electrons in a bond. Electron affinity is the energy change when a lone gas atom gains an electron. Similar sounding, different measurements. Fluorine wins electronegativity, but chlorine beats it on electron affinity, because fluorine is so tiny that its electrons crowd each other. Examiners love that one.

Worked example: rank an isoelectronic series

Rank $\ce{S^{2-}}$, $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Ca^{2+}}$ from largest to smallest.

  1. Count electrons: $16 + 2 = 18$, $17 + 1 = 18$, $19 - 1 = 18$, $20 - 2 = 18$. All four are isoelectronic.
  2. With the same electrons, more protons pull harder. The proton counts are 16, 17, 19 and 20.
  3. Fewest protons means largest, so the order is $\ce{S^{2-}} > \ce{Cl-} > \ce{K+} > \ce{Ca^{2+}}$.
  4. Data check: 184, 181, 138 and 100 pm.

Ionization energy, and its two dips

First ionization energy is the energy needed to remove the outermost electron from a gas atom: $\ce{X(g) -> X+(g) + e-}$. It always costs energy, so it is always positive. The climb across a period is not perfectly smooth, and the two bumps are favorite exam questions.

Exam trap: nitrogen beats oxygen

The trend says oxygen should have the higher ionization energy, but nitrogen does: 14.53 eV against 13.62 eV. Likewise beryllium beats boron. If a question asks you to rank these, use the real order and be ready to explain why.

Worked example: find the group from the wall

An element has successive ionization energies of 578, 1817, 2745, 11 577 and 14 842 kJ/mol. Which group is it in?

  1. Compare neighbors. From 578 to 1817 is about 3 times, and from 1817 to 2745 is about 1.5 times.
  2. From 2745 to 11 577 is more than 4 times, and it is by far the largest jump in absolute size. That is the wall.
  3. The wall comes after the third electron, so the atom has 3 valence electrons.
  4. Three valence electrons means group 13. These are the real values for aluminum.

Pocket card

  • $Z_{\text{eff}} \approx Z - \text{core}$. Across a period it rises. Down a group it stays about the same, but $n$ grows.
  • Radius: gets smaller across, bigger down.
  • Ionization energy and electronegativity: rise across a period, fall going down a group.
  • Electron affinity: generally stronger across, weaker down, with many exceptions. Cl beats F.
  • Metallic character: falls across a period, rises going down a group.
  • Cations are smaller than their atoms, and anions larger. In an isoelectronic series, more protons means smaller.
  • Dips: Be to B (2p is higher) and N to O (first pair repels). A huge jump in successive IE marks the end of the valence electrons.
