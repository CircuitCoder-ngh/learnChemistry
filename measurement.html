Measuring and significant figures

A measurement is a number, a unit, and an honest statement of how sure you are.

  • Use SI units and the prefixes from nano to mega
  • Write any number in scientific notation
  • Tell accuracy from precision, and read a scale to one extra digit
  • Count significant figures, and use the right rule for adding and for multiplying

Units and prefixes

"Five" means nothing by itself. Five what? Scientists everywhere agreed on one set of units, called SI units. You will use these five constantly.

Yes, the base unit of mass is the kilogram, which already has a prefix. Nobody is happy about this. Volume uses the liter, which is not strictly SI but is everywhere in chemistry.

Prefixes are nicknames for powers of ten, so you never have to write 0.000000001 m. Climb the ladder. Each rung has its real size, and these prefixes work on any unit: 1 mg = 0.001 g, and 1 mL = 0.001 L.

Scientific notation

Chemistry numbers are often huge or tiny. Scientific notation writes them as a number from 1 to 10, times a power of ten. The power of ten just records how far the decimal point moved. Slide the point below. The value never changes.

Scientific notation

$$a \times 10^{n} \qquad 1 \le a < 10$$
$a$
The front number, with exactly one nonzero digit before the point.
$n$
A whole number. Positive for big numbers, negative for numbers below 1.

Worked example: write it, then multiply

Write 0.00042 in scientific notation, then multiply it by $2.0 \times 10^{6}$.

  1. Move the point right 4 places to get 4.2. Moving right makes the exponent negative: $0.00042 = 4.2 \times 10^{-4}$.
  2. Multiply the front numbers: $4.2 \times 2.0 = 8.4$.
  3. Add the exponents: $10^{-4} \times 10^{6} = 10^{-4 + 6} = 10^{2}$.
  4. The answer is $8.4 \times 10^{2}$, which is 840.

Accuracy and precision

People use these words as if they meant the same thing. In chemistry they do not. Accuracy is how close you get to the true value. Precision is how close your repeated tries are to each other. Throw some darts. Can you be very precise and still be wrong?

Precise means repeatable. Accurate means right. You can have either one without the other.

Reading a scale

Every measuring tool has a limit, and that limit decides how many digits you are allowed to write down. Here is how to read one properly.

Significant figures

The digits you measured, including that one estimate, are called significant figures, or sig figs. Zeros are the tricky part, because sometimes a zero was measured and sometimes it only holds a place.

Exam trap: 100, 100. and 1.00 × 10²

These can all mean the same amount but say different things about how well it was measured. $100$ has 1 sig fig by the usual rule, though some teachers call it ambiguous. $100.$ with a decimal point has 3. $1.00 \times 10^{2}$ also has 3. Scientific notation is the way to be unmistakable.

Sig figs in calculations

A calculator happily gives you nine digits, but your answer can never be more certain than your worst measurement. There are two rules, and exams love to mix them up. Pick an operation and watch which digits survive.

The two rules

Add, subtract
Line up the decimal points. Keep the fewest decimal places of any number.
Multiply, divide
Count sig figs. Keep the fewest significant figures of any number.

Exam trap: using the wrong rule

$12.11 + 1.0$ keeps one decimal place, giving $13.1$, which has 3 sig figs even though $1.0$ has only 2. Adding looks at place, not at count. Also, exact numbers never limit you: the 2 in "2 mol of $\ce{H2O}$ per reaction" and the 1000 in "1 km = 1000 m" have unlimited sig figs.

Worked example: adding

What is $12.11 + 18.0 + 1.013$?

  1. Line up the points and add: $12.11 + 18.0 + 1.013 = 31.123$.
  2. Find the fewest decimal places. $18.0$ has only 1, so the answer may keep only 1.
  3. Round to one decimal place: $31.1$.

Worked example: multiplying

A block is 4.56 cm long and 1.4 cm wide. What is its area?

  1. Multiply: $4.56\text{ cm} \times 1.4\text{ cm} = 6.384\text{ cm}^2$.
  2. Count sig figs. $4.56$ has 3 and $1.4$ has 2, so the answer may keep only 2.
  3. Round to 2 sig figs: $6.4\text{ cm}^2$.

In a long calculation, keep extra digits all the way through and round only once, at the very end. Rounding at every step quietly adds error.

Pocket card

  • mega $10^{6}$, kilo $10^{3}$, deci $10^{-1}$, centi $10^{-2}$, milli $10^{-3}$, micro $10^{-6}$, nano $10^{-9}$.
  • Scientific notation: $a \times 10^{n}$ with $1 \le a < 10$. Point moves left, exponent goes up.
  • Accuracy is closeness to the true value. Precision is closeness to each other.
  • Read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level, and estimate one digit past the lines.
  • Nonzero digits and zeros between them count. Leading zeros never count. Trailing zeros count only with a decimal point.
  • Add or subtract: fewest decimal places. Multiply or divide: fewest sig figs. Exact numbers never limit.
