Ions and compounds

Some atoms trade electrons and stack into crystals. Others share and form separate molecules. Formulas mean something different for each.

  • Tell cations from anions, and ionic compounds from molecular ones
  • Explain why an ionic formula is a ratio and a molecular formula is a real count
  • Write the formula of any ionic compound by balancing charges
  • Recognize the common polyatomic ions

Two kinds of compound

A compound is two or more elements bonded together. There are two main ways to do that. Atoms can hand electrons over, or they can share them. The story shows both.

Remember which ion is which: a cat-ion has paws, so it is paws-itive. Yes, I know. An an-ion is a negative ion. Moles have paws too, but nobody made a word for us.

Metal plus nonmetal usually means ionic. Only nonmetals usually means molecular.

Pull on them and see the difference

Grab a particle and pull. In salt, can you find the chloride that belongs to one sodium? Then switch to water and try again.

Exam trap: formula unit or molecule?

$\ce{NaCl}$ is a formula unit: the smallest ratio of ions in the crystal, not a thing you can pick up. $\ce{H2O}$ is a molecule: a real, separate particle. So never call it "a molecule of salt." One exception to the shortcut: compounds with ammonium, such as $\ce{NH4Cl}$, are ionic even though they contain only nonmetals.

Writing ionic formulas: charges must cancel

Crystals are neutral, so the positive and negative charges must add to zero. That single rule gives you every ionic formula. Try it. Add ions until every plus has a matching minus.

The shortcut: criss-cross

Balancing by hand always works, and it shows why. Once you get it, there is a faster way. The charge of each ion becomes the subscript of the other. Then simplify if you can. Pick any pair.

The rule behind every ionic formula

$$\begin{gathered} (\text{cations})(\text{cation charge}) \\ +\ (\text{anions})(\text{anion charge}) = 0 \end{gathered}$$

Worked example: write a formula from charges

What is the formula of the compound of aluminum ions and sulfate ions?

  1. Write the ions with their charges: $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$.
  2. Find the smallest total charge both can reach. The lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6.
  3. You need $+6$: that takes 2 aluminum ions, since $2 \times (+3) = +6$.
  4. You need $-6$: that takes 3 sulfate ions, since $3 \times (-2) = -6$.
  5. More than one sulfate means parentheses: $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$. Check: $+6 - 6 = 0$.

Worked example: find a charge from a formula

What is the charge on iron in $\ce{Fe2O3}$?

  1. Oxide is always $-2$. Three of them total $3 \times (-2) = -6$.
  2. The whole formula is neutral, so the two irons must total $+6$.
  3. Divide among the irons: $\dfrac{+6}{2} = +3$. Each iron is $\ce{Fe^3+}$.

Exam trap: three formula mistakes

  • Forgetting parentheses. $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ has two hydroxides. Writing $\ce{CaOH2}$ means something else entirely.
  • Not simplifying. Criss-crossing $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$ gives $\ce{Ca2O2}$, which must reduce to $\ce{CaO}$. But never reduce inside a polyatomic ion, and never reduce a molecular formula: $\ce{H2O2}$ stays $\ce{H2O2}$.
  • Seeing a bond that is not there. The $\ce{Cl2}$ in $\ce{MgCl2}$ is not a chlorine molecule. It means two separate $\ce{Cl-}$ ions.

First look: empirical and molecular formulas

A molecular formula gives the real count of atoms in one molecule. An empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number ratio. You will calculate these from lab data in a later lesson, so for now just see what they mean.

Pocket card

  • Cations are positive and anions are negative. Metals make cations, and nonmetals make anions.
  • Ionic: metal plus nonmetal, or anything with ammonium. A crystal lattice, and the formula is a ratio.
  • Molecular: nonmetals only. Separate molecules, and the formula is a real count.
  • Ionic formulas: charges must add to zero, using the smallest whole-number ratio.
  • Use parentheses when more than one polyatomic ion appears: $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$.
  • -ate has one more oxygen than -ite. Per- adds one more, hypo- takes one more away.
  • Empirical formula: simplest ratio. Molecular formula: actual count.
