On every exam

Unit conversions and density

One trick, units that cancel like fractions, solves half of chemistry. It also tells you when you got it wrong.

  • Build conversion factors and cancel units, even across several steps
  • Convert squared and cubed units without the classic mistake
  • Use density, rearranged for any variable, and predict float or sink
  • Convert between °C, K and °F

Units cancel like fractions

Chemists call this dimensional analysis. It sounds fancy. It is really one idea: you can multiply anything by 1 without changing it, and there are many clever ways to write 1.

A conversion factor is a fraction equal to 1. Put the unit you want to lose on the bottom.

Now you do it. Tap a fraction to add it to the chain, and flip it first if it needs flipping. Watch the units: they tell you when you are done, and they tell you when you are wrong.

Exam trap: the flipped fraction

$2.50\text{ km} \times \dfrac{1\text{ km}}{1000\text{ m}}$ gives $0.00250\ \text{km}^2/\text{m}$. A calculator will not stop you, but the units will. If the units left over are not the ones you wanted, a fraction is upside down. Always write the units down.

Worked example: a chain of conversions

How many seconds are in 3.00 hours?

  1. Plan a path: hours to minutes to seconds.
  2. Put hours on the bottom to cancel them: $3.00\ \cancel{\text{h}} \times \dfrac{60\ \text{min}}{1\ \cancel{\text{h}}} = 180\ \text{min}$.
  3. Now put minutes on the bottom: $180\ \cancel{\text{min}} \times \dfrac{60\ \text{s}}{1\ \cancel{\text{min}}} = 10800\ \text{s}$.
  4. The 60s are exact, so only 3.00 limits the sig figs: $1.08 \times 10^{4}$ s.

Squared and cubed units

This is where most people get burned. A meter is 100 centimeters, but a cubic meter is not 100 cubic centimeters. Not even close.

Exam trap: forgetting to cube the number

$(100\text{ cm})^3$ means $100^3\text{ cm}^3$, which is $1{,}000{,}000\text{ cm}^3$. Cube the number and the unit. And remember this pair, since it is on every exam: $1\text{ mL} = 1\text{ cm}^3$, and $1\text{ L} = 1000\text{ mL}$.

Worked example: cubic units

A tank holds 2.5 m³. How many liters is that?

  1. Cube the factor to change m³ to cm³: $2.5\ \cancel{\text{m}^3} \times \left(\dfrac{100\text{ cm}}{1\ \cancel{\text{m}}}\right)^{3} = 2.5 \times 10^{6}\text{ cm}^3$.
  2. Use $1\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ mL}$: that is $2.5 \times 10^{6}\text{ mL}$.
  3. Change mL to L: $2.5 \times 10^{6}\ \cancel{\text{mL}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ L}}{1000\ \cancel{\text{mL}}} = $ $2.5 \times 10^{3}$ L.

Density

Density is how much mass is packed into each bit of space. It also works as a conversion factor between mass and volume, so everything you just learned still applies.

Density, three ways

$$d = \frac{m}{V} \qquad m = d \times V \qquad V = \frac{m}{d}$$
$d$
Density, usually in g/mL or g/cm³ (the same thing). Gases are often in g/L.
$m$
Mass, usually in g.
$V$
Volume, usually in mL or cm³.

Worked example: find the volume

What volume does 50.0 g of ethanol occupy? Its density is 0.789 g/mL.

  1. Rearrange for $V$: $d = \dfrac{m}{V}$, so multiply both sides by $V$ to get $dV = m$, then divide by $d$ to get $V = \dfrac{m}{d}$.
  2. Substitute, and watch grams cancel: $V = 50.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mL}}{0.789\ \cancel{\text{g}}}$.
  3. Calculate with 3 sig figs: $63.4$ mL.

Something floats when it is less dense than the liquid around it. Drag these blocks into the tank, and predict first. They are all the same size. Can you make the egg float? Can you make solid iron float?

Worked example: water displacement

A rock of mass 21.6 g raises the water in a cylinder from 30.0 mL to 38.0 mL. What is its density?

  1. The rock pushes aside its own volume of water: $V = 38.0 - 30.0 = 8.0$ mL. Subtracting keeps one decimal place, which leaves 2 sig figs.
  2. Divide: $d = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{21.6\text{ g}}{8.0\text{ mL}}$.
  3. Keep the fewest sig figs, which is 2: $2.7$ g/mL. That happens to match the density of aluminum.

Temperature: three scales

Temperature measures how fast particles are moving on average. Three scales describe the same thing. Drag the temperature and all three move together. Then take it as low as it can go.

Temperature conversions

$$T_{\text{K}} = T_{^\circ\text{C}} + 273.15 \qquad T_{^\circ\text{F}} = 1.8\,T_{^\circ\text{C}} + 32$$
Backward
$T_{^\circ\text{C}} = \dfrac{T_{^\circ\text{F}} - 32}{1.8}$. Subtract first, then divide.
Absolute zero
0 K, which is $-273.15$ °C. Nothing can be colder.

Worked example: Fahrenheit to Kelvin

A room is at 68.0 °F. What is that in kelvin?

  1. There is no direct path, so go through Celsius. Subtract first: $68.0 - 32 = 36.0$.
  2. Then divide: $\dfrac{36.0}{1.8} = 20.0$ °C. The 32 and 1.8 are exact.
  3. Add to reach kelvin: $20.0 + 273.15 = $ $293.2$ K, keeping one decimal place.

Exam trap: kelvin rules

Write K, not °K. Kelvin temperatures are never negative, so a negative answer means a mistake. A change of 10 °C is also a change of 10 K, since the steps are the same size. The 273.15 matters only for a reading, not a difference.

At $-40$ the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales agree exactly. That is the one temperature where nobody has to convert. It is also the temperature at which I stay in my hole.

Pocket card

  • A conversion factor is a fraction equal to 1. Put the unit you want to lose on the bottom.
  • Cube the number too: $1\text{ m}^3 = 10^{6}\text{ cm}^3$. Square it for areas: $1\text{ m}^2 = 10^{4}\text{ cm}^2$.
  • $1\text{ mL} = 1\text{ cm}^3$, and $1\text{ L} = 1000\text{ mL}$.
  • $d = m/V$. Less dense than the liquid floats. Volume of a lump: water displacement.
  • $T_{\text{K}} = T_{^\circ\text{C}} + 273.15$, and $T_{^\circ\text{F}} = 1.8\,T_{^\circ\text{C}} + 32$. Absolute zero is 0 K.
