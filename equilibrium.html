Bonus topic

Chemical equilibrium

Many reactions run both ways. They settle when the two directions match speed, and then they keep on running.

  • Explain why equilibrium is dynamic
  • Write K, and use Q to predict the direction
  • Solve ICE tables, including the quadratic kind
  • Predict shifts with Le Chatelier's principle

Two directions at once

So far, reactions have gone one way and stopped. Many do not. Products can turn back into reactants, and the double arrow $\ce{<=>}$ says so.

At equilibrium, the forward rate equals the reverse rate. The amounts stop changing. The molecules do not stop.

Exam trap: equal rates, not equal amounts

"At equilibrium, reactants and products are equal" is the most common wrong answer on this topic. The rates are equal. The amounts can be 99 to 1 and still be at equilibrium.

Push it and watch

Every particle below decides for itself, moment by moment, whether to flip. Nobody tells the system to balance. It balances anyway. Let it settle, then disturb it.

Notice nothing in the code says "stop at this ratio". Each particle only knows its own odds of flipping. The balance is what happens when you add up thousands of tiny decisions, a bit like a crowd in my tunnels.

K and Q

Where does a reaction settle? One number says so: the equilibrium constant, $K$. A second number, $Q$, says where things stand right now. Comparing them tells you which way the reaction will go.

The equilibrium constant, for $a\text{A} + b\text{B} \rightleftharpoons c\text{C} + d\text{D}$

$$K = \frac{[\text{C}]^c[\text{D}]^d}{[\text{A}]^a[\text{B}]^b}$$
Use
Equilibrium concentrations. Gases and dissolved species only: pure solids and liquids are left out.
$Q$
The same fraction, using the concentrations at any moment.

Worked example: which way will it go?

For $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}$, $K = 0.500$ at some temperature. A flask holds $[\ce{N2}] = 1.00$ M, $[\ce{H2}] = 1.00$ M and $[\ce{NH3}] = 2.00$ M. Which way does it run?

  1. Write $Q$. Coefficients become exponents: $Q = \dfrac{[\ce{NH3}]^2}{[\ce{N2}][\ce{H2}]^3}$.
  2. Substitute the numbers: $Q = \dfrac{(2.00)^2}{(1.00)(1.00)^3} = 4.00$.
  3. Compare: $Q = 4.00$ is bigger than $K = 0.500$. There is too much product for equilibrium.
  4. So it runs in reverse, using up $\ce{NH3}$ until $Q$ falls to 0.500.

ICE tables

Most equilibrium problems give you starting amounts and $K$, then ask where it ends up. An ICE table keeps it tidy: Initial, Change, Equilibrium. The unknown amount that reacts is called $x$.

Worked example: a perfect square

$\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}$ has $K = 50.0$ near 450 °C. You start with 1.00 M of $\ce{H2}$ and 1.00 M of $\ce{I2}$. Find the concentration of $\ce{HI}$ at equilibrium.

$\ce{H2}$$\ce{I2}$$\ce{HI}$
Initial1.001.000
Change$-x$$-x$$+2x$
Equilibrium$1.00 - x$$1.00 - x$$2x$
  1. Fill the Change row from the coefficients. One $\ce{H2}$ and one $\ce{I2}$ make two $\ce{HI}$, so the changes are $-x$, $-x$ and $+2x$.
  2. Put the bottom row into $K$: $50.0 = \dfrac{(2x)^2}{(1.00 - x)(1.00 - x)} = \left(\dfrac{2x}{1.00 - x}\right)^2$.
  3. It is a perfect square, so take the root of both sides: $7.07 = \dfrac{2x}{1.00 - x}$.
  4. Solve for $x$: $7.07 - 7.07x = 2x$, so $x = \dfrac{7.07}{9.07} = 0.780$ M.
  5. Read the bottom row: $[\ce{HI}] = 2x = $ $1.56$ M, and $[\ce{H2}] = [\ce{I2}] = 0.220$ M.
  6. Check: $\dfrac{(1.56)^2}{(0.220)(0.220)} \approx 50$. It works.

Worked example: when you need the quadratic formula

$\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}$ has $K = 0.20$ at some temperature. You start with 0.50 M of $\ce{N2O4}$ and no $\ce{NO2}$. Find both equilibrium concentrations.

  1. The table's bottom row is $[\ce{N2O4}] = 0.50 - x$ and $[\ce{NO2}] = 2x$.
  2. Put them into $K$: $0.20 = \dfrac{(2x)^2}{0.50 - x}$.
  3. Multiply out and collect terms: $0.10 - 0.20x = 4x^2$, so $4x^2 + 0.20x - 0.10 = 0$.
  4. Use the quadratic formula: $x = \dfrac{-0.20 + \sqrt{(0.20)^2 + 4(4)(0.10)}}{2(4)} = \dfrac{-0.20 + 1.281}{8} = 0.1351$ M. The minus root gives a negative $x$, so drop it.
  5. Read the bottom row: $[\ce{NO2}] = 0.27$ M and $[\ce{N2O4}] = 0.36$ M, to two significant figures.

Le Chatelier's principle

Disturb a system at equilibrium and it shifts in the direction that eases the disturbance. You saw that in the lab. Here it is with a real gas that changes color, so you can see the shift without a graph.

You do thisIt shiftsDoes $K$ change?
Add a reactant, or remove a productForwardNo
Add a product, or remove a reactantReverseNo
Squeeze a gas into less volumeToward fewer gas moleculesNo
Heat an exothermic reactionReverse, since heat acts as a productYes, $K$ falls
Heat an endothermic reactionForward, since heat acts as a reactantYes, $K$ rises
Add a catalystNo shift. It only arrives sooner.No
Add an unreactive gas at fixed volumeNo shiftNo

Exam trap: only temperature changes K

Adding $\ce{A}$ shifts the amounts, but $K$ stays put. $Q$ is what moved, and the reaction runs until $Q$ matches the same old $K$. A catalyst speeds up both directions equally, so it changes neither $K$ nor the final amounts.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • At equilibrium, forward rate equals reverse rate. Amounts are constant, but rarely equal.
  • $K$ is products over reactants, with coefficients as exponents. Leave out pure solids and liquids.
  • $Q < K$ runs forward. $Q > K$ runs in reverse. $Q = K$ is at equilibrium.
  • ICE table: Initial, Change from coefficients, Equilibrium. Put the bottom row into $K$.
  • Le Chatelier: a system eases any stress. Only temperature changes $K$.
  • Reverse a reaction: $K$ becomes $1/K$. Multiply it by $n$: $K$ becomes $K^n$.
