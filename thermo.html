Bonus topic

Entropy and free energy

Why do some things happen on their own? Energy is only half the story. The other half is spreading out.

  • Explain entropy by counting arrangements
  • Predict the sign of $\Delta S$ and calculate $\Delta S^\circ$
  • Use $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$ at any temperature
  • Explain why spontaneous does not mean fast

What happens on its own?

Hot coffee cools. Nobody has ever seen cold coffee heat itself up in a cool room. Chemistry has a word for the direction things naturally go, and a number that predicts it.

Things spread out because spread-out arrangements are overwhelmingly more common. Entropy counts those arrangements.

Watch it spread

These particles have no idea where they are going. They bounce at random. Remove the wall, and see where they end up.

Nothing is pushing the particles to the right. They just have far more ways to be spread out. My sock drawer works on the same principle.

Exam trap: the system's entropy can go down

Water freezes on its own in a freezer, and ice has less entropy than liquid water. That does not break any law. The second law is about the universe: the heat released into the surroundings raises their entropy by more than the water lost.

Entropy, by counting and by table

$$S = k \ln W$$ $$\Delta S^\circ = \sum n S^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum n S^\circ(\text{reactants})$$
$W$
The number of microstates, or arrangements
$S^\circ$
Standard entropy, in J/(mol K). A perfect crystal at 0 K has $S = 0$: this is the third law.

Worked example: $\Delta S^\circ$ from a table

Find $\Delta S^\circ$ for $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}$. Use $S^\circ$ values of 191.6 for $\ce{N2}$, 130.7 for $\ce{H2}$ and 192.5 for $\ce{NH3}$, all in J/(mol K).

  1. Predict the sign. Four gas molecules become two, so expect a negative answer.
  2. Add up the products: $2(192.5) = 385.0$ J/K.
  3. Add up the reactants: $191.6 + 3(130.7) = 191.6 + 392.1 = 583.7$ J/K.
  4. Products minus reactants: $385.0 - 583.7 = $ $-198.7$ J/K. The sign matches the prediction.

Exam trap: elements have entropy

$\Delta H^\circ_f$ and $\Delta G^\circ_f$ of an element in its standard state are zero. $S^\circ$ is not. $\ce{O2(g)}$ has $S^\circ = 205.1$ J/(mol K), so never leave it out of the sum.

Free energy: energy against spreading

Two things decide what happens: lowering energy and spreading out. Sometimes they agree. When they disagree, temperature settles the argument, and one equation captures all of it.

Gibbs free energy

$$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$$
$T$
Always in kelvin
Units
$\Delta H$ is usually in kJ and $\Delta S$ in J/K. Convert one so they match.
$\Delta H$$\Delta S$$\Delta G$Spontaneous?Example

Worked example: making ammonia

For $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}$, $\Delta H^\circ = -92.2$ kJ and $\Delta S^\circ = -198.7$ J/K. Is it spontaneous at 298 K? At what temperature does that change?

  1. Convert $\Delta S$ to match: $-198.7\ \cancel{\text{J}}/\text{K} \times \dfrac{1\text{ kJ}}{1000\ \cancel{\text{J}}} = -0.1987$ kJ/K.
  2. Substitute: $\Delta G = -92.2\text{ kJ} - (298\ \cancel{\text{K}})(-0.1987\text{ kJ}/\cancel{\text{K}})$.
  3. Watch the two minus signs: $\Delta G = -92.2 + 59.2 = $ $-33.0$ kJ. It is negative, so it is spontaneous at 298 K.
  4. Both $\Delta H$ and $\Delta S$ are negative, so heating makes it worse. The flip happens where $\Delta G = 0$, so $T = \dfrac{\Delta H}{\Delta S} = \dfrac{-92.2\text{ kJ}}{-0.1987\text{ kJ/K}} = $ $464$ K. Above that, it is not spontaneous.

Exam trap: kilojoules against joules

This is the most common error on the topic. Tables give $\Delta H$ in kJ and $\Delta S$ in J/K. If you forget to convert, the $T\Delta S$ term comes out 1000 times too big and your answer is nonsense. Also use kelvin: 25 °C is 298 K.

Spontaneous does not mean fast

This one trips up nearly everyone, because in everyday English "spontaneous" means sudden. In chemistry, it has nothing to do with speed.

$\Delta G$ tells you whether a reaction can go. It says nothing about how fast. Speed belongs to kinetics and the activation energy.

Two more uses of $\Delta G^\circ$

$$\Delta G^\circ = \sum n\Delta G^\circ_f(\text{products}) - \sum n\Delta G^\circ_f(\text{reactants})$$ $$\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$$
$R$
$8.314$ J/(mol K), so use $\Delta G^\circ$ in joules here
Signs
$\Delta G^\circ < 0$ means $K > 1$. $\Delta G^\circ > 0$ means $K < 1$. $\Delta G^\circ = 0$ means $K = 1$.

Worked example: from $\Delta G^\circ$ to $K$

Making ammonia has $\Delta G^\circ = -33.0$ kJ at 298 K. Find $K$.

  1. Rearrange $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$ to get $\ln K = \dfrac{-\Delta G^\circ}{RT}$.
  2. Convert to joules, so the units cancel with $R$: $\ln K = \dfrac{33{,}000\ \cancel{\text{J/mol}}}{(8.314\ \cancel{\text{J/(mol K)}})(298\ \cancel{\text{K}})} = 13.32$.
  3. Undo the log: $K = e^{13.32} = $ $6.1 \times 10^{5}$. A big $K$ fits a negative $\Delta G^\circ$.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Spontaneous means it can happen without continued outside help. It says nothing about speed.
  • Entropy counts arrangements: $S = k\ln W$. Gas beats liquid beats solid, and more gas molecules means more entropy.
  • Second law: every spontaneous process raises the entropy of the universe.
  • $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$. Negative is spontaneous, zero is equilibrium. Convert J to kJ, and use kelvin.
  • The flip temperature is $T = \Delta H / \Delta S$, when both have the same sign.
  • $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$. Negative $\Delta G^\circ$ means $K > 1$.
