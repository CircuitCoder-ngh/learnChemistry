Phase changes

Heat ice and its temperature climbs, then stops dead, even with the burner still on. Find out where that heat goes.

  • Read a heating curve and say what the particles are doing at each part
  • Calculate the total heat for any path, using $q = mc\Delta T$ and $q = m\Delta H$
  • Explain vapor pressure, boiling and why altitude changes the boiling point
  • Read a phase diagram, including the triple and critical points

Why the temperature stops

Temperature measures how fast particles are moving. So here is a puzzle: during melting and boiling you keep adding heat, yet the temperature does not budge. Follow 100 g of ice from $-20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to steam.

On a slope, heat makes particles move faster, so temperature rises. On a flat part, heat pulls particles away from each other, so temperature stays put.

Boiling takes almost 7 times the heat of melting. Melting only loosens molecules. Boiling has to free them completely. Also, textbooks differ a little on the specific heats of ice and steam, so use the numbers your exam gives you.

Add up the whole trip

A heating curve problem is a shopping list. Cut the trip into pieces at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$, price each piece with the right formula, and add. Set any start and end here.

Worked example: ice to steam

How much heat does it take to turn 25.0 g of ice at $-10.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ into steam at $110.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$?

  1. Warm the ice to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$: $25.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times 2.09\ \dfrac{\text{J}}{\cancel{\text{g}}\ \cancel{^\circ\text{C}}} \times 10.0\ \cancel{^\circ\text{C}} = 523\text{ J}$.
  2. Melt it: $25.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times 334\ \dfrac{\text{J}}{\cancel{\text{g}}} = 8350\text{ J}$.
  3. Warm the water to $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$: $25.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times 4.18\ \dfrac{\text{J}}{\cancel{\text{g}}\ \cancel{^\circ\text{C}}} \times 100.0\ \cancel{^\circ\text{C}} = 10450\text{ J}$.
  4. Boil it: $25.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times 2260\ \dfrac{\text{J}}{\cancel{\text{g}}} = 56500\text{ J}$.
  5. Warm the steam to $110\ ^\circ\text{C}$: $25.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times 2.0\ \dfrac{\text{J}}{\cancel{\text{g}}\ \cancel{^\circ\text{C}}} \times 10.0\ \cancel{^\circ\text{C}} = 500\text{ J}$.
  6. Add them: $523 + 8350 + 10450 + 56500 + 500 = 76323\text{ J}$, which is about 76.3 kJ. Notice that boiling alone is almost three quarters of the bill.

Worked example: using moles

How much heat is released when 36.0 g of steam at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ condenses to liquid at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$? Use $\Delta H_\text{vap} = 40.7$ kJ/mol.

  1. Convert to moles: $36.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{18.02\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 2.00\text{ mol}$.
  2. Condensing is boiling in reverse, so the size is the same and the sign flips: $\Delta H_\text{cond} = -40.7$ kJ/mol.
  3. Multiply: $2.00\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \left(-40.7\ \dfrac{\text{kJ}}{\cancel{\text{mol}}}\right) = -81.4\text{ kJ}$.
  4. So 81.4 kJ of heat is released. This is why a steam burn is worse than a burn from boiling water at the same temperature.

Exam trap: never use $q = mc\Delta T$ across a phase change

Going from ice at $-10\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to water at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is not one $\Delta T$ of 30 degrees. It is three pieces: warm the ice, melt it, warm the water. Each piece has its own number. Also watch your units: $c$ and $\Delta H$ per gram use J, while $\Delta H$ per mole uses kJ.

Vapor pressure and boiling

A puddle dries up well below $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so molecules must be able to escape at any temperature. Close the lid and something interesting happens.

Clausius-Clapeyron: how vapor pressure depends on temperature

$$\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = -\frac{\Delta H_\text{vap}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)$$

The idea: vapor pressure grows very quickly with temperature, because each small warming lets many more molecules escape. Plot $\ln P$ against $1/T$ and the curve straightens into a line whose slope is $-\Delta H_\text{vap}/R$. Stickier liquids have steeper lines. Switch the graph above to see it. $T$ must be in kelvin, and $R$ is $8.314$ J/(mol K).

Worked example: vapor pressure at a new temperature

Water has a vapor pressure of 760 torr at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Estimate its vapor pressure at $80\ ^\circ\text{C}$, using $\Delta H_\text{vap} = 40.7$ kJ/mol.

  1. Change to kelvin: $T_1 = 373.15$ K and $T_2 = 353.15$ K. Change to joules: $\Delta H_\text{vap} = 40700$ J/mol.
  2. Find the bracket: $\dfrac{1}{353.15} - \dfrac{1}{373.15} = 0.00283166 - 0.00267989 = 0.00015177\text{ K}^{-1}$.
  3. Multiply: $-\dfrac{40700}{8.314} \times 0.00015177 = -0.7430$, so $\ln\dfrac{P_2}{760} = -0.7430$.
  4. Undo the log: $\dfrac{P_2}{760} = e^{-0.7430} = 0.4757$, so $P_2 = 0.4757 \times 760 =$ about 362 torr. The measured value is 355 torr, so this is a good estimate.

Exam trap: more liquid does not mean more vapor pressure

Vapor pressure depends only on the substance and the temperature. A bigger jar or more liquid does not change it. The normal boiling point is the temperature where vapor pressure equals exactly 1 atm, which is 760 torr. A stronger flame also does not make water hotter: at 1 atm it stays at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and just boils faster.

Phase diagrams

A phase diagram is a map. Give it a temperature and a pressure, and it tells you which phase you get. Learn the parts first, then drive around on it yourself.

Exam trap: which way does the line lean?

For almost everything, the solid-liquid line leans to the right, because squeezing favors the denser solid. Water is the odd one: its line leans to the left, because liquid water is denser than ice, so squeezing ice can melt it. To get a liquid, the pressure must be above the triple point pressure. That is why $\ce{CO2}$ at 1 atm goes straight from solid to gas.

Pocket card

  • On a slope use $q = mc\Delta T$. On a flat part use $q = m\Delta H$ or $q = n\Delta H$. Temperature is constant during a phase change.
  • Water: $c$ is 2.09 (ice), 4.18 (liquid), 2.0 (steam) J/(g °C). $\Delta H_\text{fus}$ is 334 J/g, and $\Delta H_\text{vap}$ is 2260 J/g.
  • Vapor pressure is the pressure of vapor in equilibrium with its liquid. It rises with temperature and falls with stronger forces.
  • A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the outside pressure.
  • $\ln(P_2/P_1) = -\frac{\Delta H_\text{vap}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2}-\frac{1}{T_1}\right)$, with $T$ in kelvin.
  • Triple point: all three phases together. Critical point: past it, liquid and gas become one supercritical fluid.
