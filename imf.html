On every exam

Intermolecular forces

Molecules are a little bit sticky. That stickiness decides what boils, what floats, and why water climbs a paper towel.

  • Tell a bond inside a molecule from a force between molecules
  • Find which forces any substance has, and which is strongest
  • Rank boiling points and explain the order
  • Explain surface tension, viscosity, capillary action and floating ice

Inside or between?

There are two very different kinds of holding together. Bonds hold atoms together inside a molecule. Intermolecular forces hold whole molecules near each other. Mixing these up is the most common mistake in this whole unit, so let us boil some water and catch it in the act.

Exam trap: boiling does not break bonds

The bubbles in boiling water are water vapor, not hydrogen and oxygen. Every molecule leaves whole. So when a question asks "what must be overcome to boil water?", the answer is hydrogen bonds between molecules, never "O–H bonds".

Bonds are inside molecules and strong. Intermolecular forces are between molecules and weak. Melting and boiling only fight the weak ones.

The four forces

All intermolecular forces come from the same rule: opposite charges attract. They differ in where the charges come from. Step through them from weakest to strongest.

ForceWho has itWhere the pull comes from
London dispersionEverythingElectrons slosh, making a brief dipole. Bigger electron clouds pull harder.
Dipole-dipolePolar moleculesA permanent plus end attracts a neighbor's permanent minus end.
Hydrogen bondingH bonded directly to N, O or FA very bare H is pulled to a lone pair on the next molecule's N, O or F.
Ion-dipoleAn ion in a polar liquidA full charge attracts the opposite end of polar molecules.

Three questions find the force

Ask them in this order for any molecule. Every molecule gets London dispersion for free. Then ask whether it is polar, then whether it has an H on N, O or F. Try the tricky ones here: $\ce{CO2}$ has polar bonds but is not polar, and $\ce{CH3F}$ has both H and F but the H is on carbon.

Exam trap: the H has to be on N, O or F

$\ce{CH3F}$ contains hydrogen and fluorine, but every H sits on carbon, so it has no hydrogen bonding. $\ce{CH3OH}$ does, because one H sits on oxygen. Always look at what the H is bonded to.

Stickiness sets the boiling point

Boiling is molecules escaping their neighbors. The stickier the molecules, the more heat it takes before they can break free. Here is a simple simulation where each substance gets stickiness matched to its real boiling point. At 25 °C, can you find one substance that is a gas, one that is a liquid, and one that is a solid?

To rank boiling points, first sort by the strongest force. If two substances tie on force, the bigger one wins, because it has a bigger electron cloud. Bigger always helps, and yes, I take this personally.

Worked example: ranking three molecules

Put $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{H2S}$ and $\ce{H2Se}$ in order of boiling point, and explain the surprise.

  1. Find the strongest force in each. $\ce{H2O}$ has H on O, so hydrogen bonding. $\ce{H2S}$ and $\ce{H2Se}$ are polar with no H on N, O or F, so dipole-dipole.
  2. Compare the two without hydrogen bonds. Both have dipole-dipole forces, and $\ce{H2Se}$ is bigger, so its London forces are stronger. $\ce{H2Se}$ boils higher than $\ce{H2S}$.
  3. Place water. Hydrogen bonding is so much stronger that the lightest molecule boils highest. Real data: $\ce{H2S}$ at $-60\ ^\circ\text{C}$, $\ce{H2Se}$ at $-41\ ^\circ\text{C}$, $\ce{H2O}$ at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
  4. The order is $\ce{H2S} < \ce{H2Se} < \ce{H2O}$. Without hydrogen bonds, water would be expected to boil well below zero.

Worked example: the energy to boil versus to break

How much energy does it take to boil 18.0 g of water? How does this compare with breaking its O–H bonds? Use $\Delta H_\text{vap} = 40.7$ kJ/mol and an O–H bond enthalpy of 463 kJ/mol.

  1. Find the moles: $18.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{18.02\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 1.00\text{ mol}$.
  2. Boiling: $1.00\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times 40.7\ \dfrac{\text{kJ}}{\cancel{\text{mol}}} = 40.7\text{ kJ}$.
  3. Each molecule has 2 O–H bonds, so 1.00 mol of water has 2.00 mol of bonds: $2.00\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times 463\ \dfrac{\text{kJ}}{\cancel{\text{mol}}} = 926\text{ kJ}$.
  4. Compare: $926 \div 40.7 \approx 23$. Breaking the bonds takes about 23 times more energy than boiling.

Why ice floats

Almost every solid sinks in its own liquid. Ice floats. Hydrogen bonds are the reason. Each molecule in ice holds its neighbors at arm's length, in an open pattern with gaps. Melt it and the pattern collapses, so the same molecules fit in less room. Same mass, less volume, higher density.

What stickiness does to a liquid

Three everyday behaviors of liquids all come from intermolecular forces. Cohesion means a liquid sticking to itself. Adhesion means a liquid sticking to something else, such as glass.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Bonds are inside molecules. Intermolecular forces are between them, and are far weaker.
  • Melting and boiling overcome intermolecular forces. Molecules stay whole.
  • Every molecule has London dispersion. Polar molecules add dipole-dipole. H on N, O or F adds hydrogen bonding. Ions in polar liquids feel ion-dipole.
  • Stronger forces mean higher boiling point, viscosity and surface tension, and lower vapor pressure.
  • For a tie on force, the bigger electron cloud wins.
  • Ice floats because hydrogen bonds hold it in an open pattern, so it is less dense than liquid water.
