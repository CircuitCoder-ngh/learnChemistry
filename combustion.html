Combustion equations

Fire is atoms swapping partners with oxygen. Learn one three-step recipe and you can balance any fuel on any exam.

  • Say what combustion is and name what a fire needs
  • Predict the products of complete and incomplete combustion
  • Balance any fuel: carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen, then clear fractions
  • Work out grams of $\ce{CO2}$, grams of $\ce{O2}$, and heat released

What fire really is

A flame looks like a thing. It is not. It is something happening: a chemical reaction, fast enough that you can see and feel it.

Combustion is a fuel reacting rapidly with $\ce{O2}$, releasing heat and light.

Rust is iron reacting with oxygen too, but it takes years and gives no flame. The word "rapidly" is what separates them. A fire also has needs. Take away any one of three things and it dies. Chemists call them the fire triangle.

Methane, atom by atom

Now the real thing. The gas in a kitchen stove or a lab burner is mostly methane, $\ce{CH4}$. Watch it burn in three views at once: what you see, what the particles do, and what chemists write.

Nothing vanishes in a fire. Every atom that went in comes out, wearing a new outfit. If your equation loses an atom, the universe will not be impressed, and neither will your grader.

Any fuel made of C and H, or of C, H and O, gives $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ when it burns completely. Always those two.

Exam trap: hydrogen leaves as water

Students under pressure often write $\ce{H2}$ as a product. Hydrogen gas is itself a fuel, so it could not survive a fire. Hydrogen always leaves as $\ce{H2O}$. And never forget $\ce{O2}$ on the left: without it, you have not written a burn at all.

The recipe: C, then H, then O last

Here is why the order matters. Carbon appears in only one product, and so does hydrogen, so their numbers are forced. Oxygen appears in both products, so leave it for the end. $\ce{O2}$ contains nothing but oxygen, which means you can adjust it last without breaking anything you already fixed.

The three-step recipe

$\text{C first} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{H next} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{O last} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{clear any fraction}$

When oxygen needs a fraction

Half the fuels you meet will land you on a fraction. It is not a mistake. It is a normal stop on the way, and one quick move fixes it.

Exam trap: the fraction left behind

Most exams want the smallest whole numbers, so $\ce{C2H6 + 7/2 O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{2CO2 + 3H2O}$ loses marks. Double every coefficient, including the invisible 1 in front of the fuel. The one exception: enthalpy problems sometimes want "per 1 mol of fuel", and there the fraction is correct. Read the question.

Exam trap: oxygen lives in two places

On the right, O sits in both $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$, and you must add both. In $\ce{4CO2 + 6H2O}$ that is $4 \times 2 + 6 \times 1 = 14$. Counting only the $\ce{CO2}$ gives 8, and counting $\ce{H2O}$ as 2 O each gives 20. Both are wrong.

The alkane family

Alkanes are hydrocarbons with only single bonds. Every one follows the same pattern: $C_nH_{2n+2}$, so once you know the carbons, you know the hydrogens. Slide along the family, and look at the oxygen count. Which ones need doubling?

Any alkane, with 1 fuel molecule

$$\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}} + \frac{3n+1}{2}\,\ce{O2} \;\longrightarrow\; n\,\ce{CO2} + (n+1)\,\ce{H2O}$$
$n$
number of carbon atoms. If the oxygen term is a fraction, double every term.

Fuels that bring their own oxygen

Methanol, ethanol and glucose already contain O. The products are still $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$, and the recipe is the same, with one extra subtraction at the end.

Worked example: methanol hits a fraction

Balance the complete combustion of methanol, $\ce{CH3OH}$.

  1. Skeleton: $\ce{CH3OH + O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{CO2 + H2O}$.
  2. Carbon. 1 C on the left, so $\ce{1CO2}$.
  3. Hydrogen. Count them all: 3 on the carbon plus 1 on the oxygen makes 4 H. $4 \div 2 = 2$, so $\ce{2H2O}$.
  4. Oxygen. The right has $1 \times 2 + 2 \times 1 = 4$ O. The fuel brings 1, so $\ce{O2}$ must bring 3. $3 \div 2 = \tfrac{3}{2}$, so write $\tfrac{3}{2}\ce{O2}$.
  5. Clear the fraction. Double everything: $\ce{2CH3OH + 3O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{2CO2 + 4H2O}$.
  6. Check. C: 2 and 2. H: 8 and 8. O: $2 + 6 = 8$ and $4 + 4 = 8$. Balanced.

When oxygen runs short

Everything so far assumed plenty of air. Starve a flame and the carbon cannot finish its job. It stops at carbon monoxide, $\ce{CO}$, or even at bare carbon, soot. This is called incomplete combustion. A Bunsen burner lets you control it with a sliding air hole.

Look at the heat readout as you close the hole. A sooty flame wastes fuel: the carbon that stops at $\ce{CO}$ or C still has energy locked inside it. That is why a stove with a yellow flame needs fixing.

Burning without carbon

Fuel does not have to contain carbon. Hydrogen, magnesium and sulfur all burn in oxygen. The rule becomes simple: you get the oxide. No carbon in, no $\ce{CO2}$ out.

Comparing fuels

Not all fuels are equal. Octane makes eight $\ce{CO2}$ per molecule and methane makes one, which sounds like a huge gap. Now switch to "per gram" and watch the gap shrink. Which measure is fair depends on the question you ask.

The mass surprise, and the math

A car burns about 3 kg of gasoline and makes about 9 kg of $\ce{CO2}$. That sounds impossible until you remember that most of the mass of $\ce{CO2}$ is oxygen pulled from the air. Mass in always equals mass out. Fuel is just not all of the mass in.

Worked example: grams of $\ce{CO2}$ from propane

What mass of $\ce{CO2}$ forms when 10.0 g of propane burns completely?

  1. Balance it first: $\ce{C3H8 + 5O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{3CO2 + 4H2O}$.
  2. Molar mass of $\ce{C3H8}$: $3(12.01) + 8(1.008) = 44.09\text{ g/mol}$.
  3. Grams to moles: $10.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{44.09\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 0.227\text{ mol C}_3\text{H}_8$.
  4. Mole ratio from the coefficients: $0.227\ \cancel{\text{mol C}_3\text{H}_8} \times \dfrac{3\text{ mol CO}_2}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol C}_3\text{H}_8}} = 0.680\text{ mol CO}_2$.
  5. Moles to grams: $0.680\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{44.01\text{ g}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = $ $29.9\text{ g CO}_2$. Three sig figs, because 10.0 has three.

Worked example: oxygen and heat for methanol

How many grams of $\ce{O2}$ does it take to burn 5.00 g of methanol? Burning 1 mol releases about 726 kJ. How much heat comes out?

  1. Use the equation from before: $\ce{2CH3OH + 3O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{2CO2 + 4H2O}$.
  2. Molar mass of $\ce{CH3OH}$ is $12.01 + 4(1.008) + 16.00 = 32.04\text{ g/mol}$, so $5.00\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{32.04\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 0.156\text{ mol}$.
  3. Mole ratio, using the coefficients 3 and 2: $0.156\ \cancel{\text{mol CH}_3\text{OH}} \times \dfrac{3\text{ mol O}_2}{2\ \cancel{\text{mol CH}_3\text{OH}}} = 0.234\text{ mol O}_2$.
  4. Mass of oxygen: $0.234\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{32.00\text{ g}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = $ $7.49\text{ g O}_2$.
  5. Heat: $0.156\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times 726\text{ kJ/}\cancel{\text{mol}} = $ $113\text{ kJ}$.

The heat side of this story continues in enthalpy and Hess's law. The grams side continues in stoichiometry. And chemists run this whole lesson backward, burning an unknown compound and weighing the $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ to work out its formula. That is combustion analysis.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Combustion: fuel + $\ce{O2}$, fast, giving heat and light. A fire needs fuel, oxygen and heat.
  • Fuels with C and H, or C, H and O, burn completely to $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$. Never $\ce{H2}$.
  • Balance in this order: C, then H, then O last. Add O from both products, and subtract any O in the fuel.
  • Fraction on $\ce{O2}$? Multiply every coefficient by the denominator.
  • Short of oxygen: $\ce{CO}$ or soot (C) instead of $\ce{CO2}$, with less heat. $\ce{CO}$ is a silent poison.
  • Elements burn to their oxides: $\ce{2H2 + O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{2H2O}$, $\ce{2Mg + O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{2MgO}$, $\ce{S + O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{SO2}$.
