Solubility rules

Some salts dissolve away in water and some refuse. A short list of rules tells you which, before you ever touch a beaker.

  • Say what soluble and insoluble really mean, down at the particles
  • Predict whether any common ionic compound dissolves
  • Name the exceptions: silver, lead, mercury(I) and friends
  • Back up every answer by naming the rule behind it

Dissolving is a tug of war

An ionic solid is a crowd of positive and negative ions holding on to each other. Water pulls on those same ions. So every ion on the edge of a crystal feels two pulls at once. Watch who wins.

If water's pull beats the crystal's grip, the compound dissolves. If the crystal wins, it stays solid.

Try it with six compounds. Some of them surprise people, because high charges alone do not settle it. That is exactly why chemists keep a list of rules instead of a formula.

Insoluble does not mean zero

Here is the honest version. Every ionic compound dissolves a little. We call something soluble when more than about 0.01 mol dissolves per liter, and insoluble when less does. The line is a rule of thumb, not a wall.

Exam trap: dissolving is not breaking into atoms

$\ce{Na2SO4}$ dissolves into $\ce{2Na+}$ and one whole $\ce{SO4^2-}$. Polyatomic ions stay in one piece. Sulfate does not fall apart into sulfur and oxygen, and the sodium ions do not stay paired.

The rules, in an order that sticks

Six rules cover everything on a first-year exam. They come in three tiers. Learn the top tier first, because it wins every argument.

Always soluble

Rule 1 Check the cation

$\ce{Li+}$$\ce{Na+}$$\ce{K+}$$\ce{NH4+}$

All of group 1, plus ammonium. Beats every other rule.

Rule 2 Check the anion

$\ce{NO3-}$$\ce{C2H3O2-}$$\ce{ClO3-}$$\ce{ClO4-}$

Nitrate, acetate, chlorate, perchlorate.

Usually soluble

Rule 3 Halides

$\ce{Cl-}$$\ce{Br-}$$\ce{I-}$

Except $\ce{Ag+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$, $\ce{Hg2^2+}$.

Rule 4 Sulfate

$\ce{SO4^2-}$

Except $\ce{Ba^2+}$, $\ce{Sr^2+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$, $\ce{Hg2^2+}$. Only slightly soluble with $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Ag+}$.

Usually insoluble

Rule 5 The stubborn three

$\ce{CO3^2-}$$\ce{PO4^3-}$$\ce{S^2-}$

Except with group 1 and ammonium.

Rule 6 Hydroxide

$\ce{OH-}$

Except with group 1 and ammonium. $\ce{Ba^2+}$ dissolves well. $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Sr^2+}$ only slightly.

Four memory hooks. NAG SAG: Nitrates, Acetates, Group 1, Sulfates, Ammonium, group 17 (chloride, bromide, iodide) all dissolve. The grumpy three ruin the party: silver, lead, mercury(I). CaStroBear (calcium, strontium, barium) trouble sulfates too. And COPS say OH: carbonate, oxide, phosphate, sulfide, hydroxide stay solid.

Knowing the rules is half of it. The other half is asking in the right order, because an early rule can overrule a later one.

Exam trap: judging by the anion alone

"Carbonates are insoluble, so $\ce{Na2CO3}$ is insoluble." Wrong, and tests love it. Always look at the cation first. If it is group 1 or ammonium, you are done: it dissolves. The same goes for $\ce{NaOH}$, $\ce{K3PO4}$ and $\ce{(NH4)2S}$.

Walk a compound through the rules

On an exam, give the answer and the reason. Each check below takes about two seconds once you are used to it.

Worked example: a tricky mix of ions

Is $\ce{Pb(NO3)2}$ soluble? Lead is one of the grumpy three.

  1. Split it into its ions: $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$.
  2. Rule 1: is the cation group 1 or ammonium? No, it is lead. Keep going.
  3. Rule 2: is the anion nitrate, acetate, chlorate or perchlorate? Yes, nitrate.
  4. Stop there. It is soluble. Lead only spoils halides and sulfates, not nitrates.

Worked example: a real exception

Is $\ce{SrSO4}$ soluble?

  1. Split it into its ions: $\ce{Sr^2+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$.
  2. Rule 1: strontium is in group 2, not group 1. Keep going.
  3. Rule 2: sulfate is not on the always list. Keep going.
  4. Rule 3: not a halide. Keep going.
  5. Rule 4: sulfate usually dissolves, but strontium is an exception (CaStroBear). It is insoluble.

Will it dissolve?

Now do it fast. Formulas fall toward the beaker. Decide before they land. Get three wrong and the round ends. Every answer tells you the rule, so wrong answers teach too.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Soluble means more than about 0.01 M dissolves. Insoluble means less. Neither means zero.
  • Ask the cation first: group 1 or ammonium dissolves with everything.
  • Nitrate, acetate, chlorate, perchlorate always dissolve.
  • Halides dissolve, except with $\ce{Ag+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$, $\ce{Hg2^2+}$.
  • Sulfates dissolve, except with $\ce{Ba^2+}$, $\ce{Sr^2+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$, $\ce{Hg2^2+}$, and only slightly with $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Ag+}$.
  • Carbonates, phosphates, sulfides, hydroxides stay solid, unless rule 1 rescues them. $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$ dissolves. $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ and $\ce{Sr(OH)2}$ only slightly.
