Precipitation and net ionic equations

Mix two clear liquids and a solid appears out of nowhere. Learn to predict it, and to write down what really happened.

  • Predict whether two solutions will form a precipitate
  • Write molecular, complete ionic and net ionic equations
  • Pick out the spectator ions
  • Work out how many grams of solid you will get

Ions swap partners

This reaction looks like magic, but nothing new arrives. The ions were all there already, floating around in the two solutions. Mixing just lets them meet someone new. This uses the solubility rules from the last lesson.

A precipitate forms when two ions from different solutions meet and cannot stay dissolved together.

Mix any two solutions

Here is a shelf with 18 solutions. Each one dissolves on its own. Choose any two and pour. The rules decide what happens, and the equations below the stage are worked out from the charges. Colors are the real colors of these solids.

Try $\ce{KNO3}$ with anything. Nothing ever happens. Potassium and nitrate are the two most easygoing ions in chemistry, which also makes them very dull at parties.

Three ways to write the same event

Chemists write this reaction three ways. Each one tells you something different.

The molecular equation shows whole compounds, which is what you would measure out of bottles. The complete ionic equation splits everything that is really split up in water. The net ionic equation drops the ions that did nothing, called spectators, leaving only what changed.

Now step through it yourself. Pick a reaction, and watch each move on its own.

Example: all three forms

$$\ce{AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) -> AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)}$$ $$\ce{Ag+(aq) + NO3^-(aq) + Na+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) -> AgCl(s) + Na+(aq) + NO3^-(aq)}$$ $$\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}$$

Exam trap: the subscript does not stay attached

When $\ce{Pb(NO3)2}$ splits, it makes one $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and two $\ce{NO3-}$, not one $\ce{(NO3)2^2-}$. The subscript becomes a coefficient. Likewise $\ce{Na2SO4}$ gives $\ce{2Na+ + SO4^2-}$.

What to split, and what to keep whole

Writing the complete ionic equation comes down to one question for each formula: is this really floating around as separate ions? Only strong electrolytes are.

Exam trap: splitting the precipitate

Students often write $\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl^-(aq)}$ on both sides. The whole point is that those ions are no longer free. Anything marked (s), (l) or (g) stays as a whole formula, on either side of the arrow.

How much solid will you get?

Precipitation is stoichiometry in disguise. Volume times molarity gives moles. Then the balanced equation takes you to the solid, just as in the stoichiometry lesson.

Moles from a solution

$$\text{moles} = \text{molarity} \times \text{liters}$$

Worked example: excess reagent

Excess $\ce{NaCl}$ solution is added to 25.0 mL of 0.200 M $\ce{AgNO3}$. What mass of $\ce{AgCl}$ forms?

  1. Write the equation: $\ce{AgNO3(aq)}$ + $\ce{NaCl(aq)}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{AgCl(s)}$ + $\ce{NaNO3(aq)}$.
  2. Convert milliliters to liters: $25.0\ \cancel{\text{mL}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ L}}{1000\ \cancel{\text{mL}}} = 0.0250\text{ L}$.
  3. Find moles of $\ce{AgNO3}$: $0.0250\ \cancel{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{0.200\text{ mol}}{1\ \cancel{\text{L}}} = 5.00 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$.
  4. The mole ratio is 1 to 1, so $5.00 \times 10^{-3}$ mol of $\ce{AgCl}$ forms.
  5. Molar mass of $\ce{AgCl}$: $107.87 + 35.45 = 143.32\text{ g/mol}$.
  6. Mass: $5.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{143.32\text{ g}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} =$ 0.717 g of $\ce{AgCl}$.

Worked example: a 1 to 2 ratio, with a limiting reagent

50.0 mL of 0.100 M $\ce{Pb(NO3)2}$ is mixed with 30.0 mL of 0.200 M $\ce{KI}$. What mass of $\ce{PbI2}$ forms?

  1. Write the equation: $\ce{Pb(NO3)2(aq)}$ + $\ce{2KI(aq)}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{PbI2(s)}$ + $\ce{2KNO3(aq)}$.
  2. Moles of $\ce{Pb(NO3)2}$: $0.0500\text{ L} \times 0.100\text{ mol/L} = 5.00 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$.
  3. Moles of $\ce{KI}$: $0.0300\text{ L} \times 0.200\text{ mol/L} = 6.00 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$.
  4. Divide each by its coefficient: $5.00 \times 10^{-3} \div 1 = 5.00 \times 10^{-3}$ and $6.00 \times 10^{-3} \div 2 = 3.00 \times 10^{-3}$. The smaller is $\ce{KI}$, so it runs out first.
  5. Moles of $\ce{PbI2}$: $6.00 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol KI} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol PbI}_2}{2\text{ mol KI}} = 3.00 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$.
  6. Molar mass of $\ce{PbI2}$: $207.2 + 2(126.90) = 461.0\text{ g/mol}$.
  7. Mass: $3.00 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol} \times 461.0\text{ g/mol} =$ 1.38 g of $\ce{PbI2}$.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • To predict a precipitate, swap partners, then check each new pair with the solubility rules.
  • Molecular: whole compounds. Complete ionic: dissolved strong electrolytes split up. Net ionic: spectators removed.
  • Split only strong electrolytes marked (aq). Never split (s), (l) or (g), or weak acids and bases.
  • Spectators are the same on both sides. Cross them out.
  • A correct net ionic equation balances atoms and charge.
  • If every ion is a spectator, there is no reaction.
