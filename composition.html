Formulas from data

A formula tells you the percentages. Run it backward, and percentages tell you the formula. That is how chemists identify an unknown.

  • Find the percent by mass of each element in a compound
  • Turn percentages into an empirical formula
  • Find a molecular formula from a molar mass
  • Work out a formula from a combustion analysis

Percent composition

What is water mostly made of? It depends on how you measure. Count the atoms and you get one answer. Weigh them and you get a very different one, and chemistry nearly always means the weight.

Percent by mass

$$\%\text{ of element} = \frac{n \times \text{atomic mass}}{\text{molar mass of compound}} \times 100\%$$
$n$
how many atoms of that element are in the formula

Now build your own. Tap atoms to add them, or start from a real compound. Watch how one heavy atom takes over the pie. Can you make a compound where hydrogen gets more than half of the mass?

Exam trap: mass, not count

Water is two thirds hydrogen by count, but only 11.2% hydrogen by mass. And use the whole formula: in $\ce{Fe2O3}$, the top of the fraction for iron is $2 \times 55.85$, not $55.85$.

From percentages to a formula

Labs do not hand you formulas. They hand you percentages. An empirical formula is the smallest whole-number ratio of atoms, and getting from one to the other is always the same four moves. A little rhyme helps: percent to mass, mass to mole, divide by small, multiply till whole.

Subscripts count atoms, and moles count atoms. So the mole ratio is the subscript ratio. Grams will never give you a formula directly.

Exam trap: never round 1.5 to 2

After dividing, a value like 1.50 is telling you to multiply, not round. Multiply all the numbers by the same whole number until they are all whole. Numbers within about 0.05 of a whole number can be rounded, since lab data has some error.

Worked example: when you must multiply

An iron oxide is 69.94% Fe and 30.06% O by mass. Find its empirical formula.

  1. Percent to mass. Assume 100 g, so you have 69.94 g Fe and 30.06 g O.
  2. Mass to mole. Fe: $69.94\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{55.85\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 1.252\text{ mol}$. O: $30.06\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{16.00\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 1.879\text{ mol}$.
  3. Divide by small. Fe: $1.252 \div 1.252 = 1$. O: $1.879 \div 1.252 = 1.50$.
  4. Multiply till whole. 1.50 is not whole, so multiply both by 2. That gives $2 : 3$, so the formula is $\ce{Fe2O3}$.

From empirical to molecular

$\ce{CH2O}$ is an empirical formula, but it could describe formaldehyde, vinegar's acetic acid, or glucose. They all share the 1:2:1 ratio. To tell which one you have, you need one more fact: the molar mass. Then count how many empirical units it takes.

How many empirical units?

$$n = \frac{\text{molar mass of the compound}}{\text{mass of one empirical formula unit}}$$
Then
multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by $n$.

Combustion analysis

Here is how an unknown compound of C, H and maybe O gets identified. Burn a weighed sample in pure oxygen. All its carbon ends up as $\ce{CO2}$ and all its hydrogen as $\ce{H2O}$, as you saw in combustion equations. Trap each gas in its own tube, and weigh the tubes.

Why is water trapped first? The $\ce{CO2}$ absorber is a base that also soaks up water. Put it first and you could not tell which gas added which grams. The order is the whole trick.

Worked example: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen

Burning 1.000 g of a compound of C, H and O gives 1.911 g of $\ce{CO2}$ and 1.173 g of $\ce{H2O}$. Find its empirical formula.

  1. Carbon. All of it is in the $\ce{CO2}$: $1.911\ \cancel{\text{g CO}_2} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol CO}_2}{44.01\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 0.04342\text{ mol}$, and each $\ce{CO2}$ has 1 C, so 0.04342 mol C. Mass: $0.04342 \times 12.01 = 0.5215\text{ g C}$.
  2. Hydrogen. $1.173\ \cancel{\text{g H}_2\text{O}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol H}_2\text{O}}{18.02\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 0.06509\text{ mol H}_2\text{O}$. Each water has 2 H, so 0.1302 mol H. Mass: $0.1302 \times 1.008 = 0.1312\text{ g H}$.
  3. Oxygen, by subtraction. $1.000 - 0.5215 - 0.1312 = 0.3473\text{ g O}$, and $0.3473 \div 16.00 = 0.02171\text{ mol O}$.
  4. Divide by small. C: $0.04342 \div 0.02171 = 2.00$. H: $0.1302 \div 0.02171 = 6.00$. O: 1.
  5. The empirical formula is $\ce{C2H6O}$, which happens to match ethanol.

Exam trap: two hydrogens per water, and oxygen by difference

Forgetting the 2 is the classic error: moles of H is twice moles of $\ce{H2O}$. And you can never read the sample's oxygen from the $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$, because most of that oxygen came from the $\ce{O2}$ supply. Find it by subtracting C and H from the sample's mass.

Pocket card

  • Percent by mass = $n \times$ atomic mass $\div$ molar mass $\times$ 100.
  • Empirical formula: percent to mass, mass to mole, divide by small, multiply till whole.
  • Never round 1.5, 1.33 or 1.25. Multiply everything by 2, 3 or 4.
  • Molecular formula: $n$ = molar mass $\div$ empirical mass, then multiply the subscripts by $n$.
  • Combustion analysis: mol C = mol $\ce{CO2}$. Mol H = 2 $\times$ mol $\ce{H2O}$. O comes from subtracting.
