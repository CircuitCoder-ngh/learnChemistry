The ideal gas law

Four numbers describe a gas. Know any three and one short equation hands you the fourth.

  • Solve $PV = nRT$ for any variable, with the right $R$ and units
  • Use the combined gas law, STP and molar volume
  • Find gas density, molar mass, and do gas stoichiometry
  • Use partial pressures, mole fractions and gas collected over water

Four laws become one equation

In the last lesson each gas law held two things still. Real problems do not always do that. So we stack the laws together into one equation that covers everything at once.

$PV = nRT$ links pressure, volume, moles and Kelvin temperature. It is the only gas equation you truly have to memorize.

The ideal gas law

$$PV = nRT$$
$$R = 0.08206\ \frac{\text{L atm}}{\text{mol K}} = 8.314\ \frac{\text{J}}{\text{mol K}}$$

Solve for anything

Pick the unknown, type what you know, and read the full working underneath. It converts your units first, exactly the way you should on paper.

Worked example: finding pressure

What pressure does 0.500 mol of $\ce{N2}$ exert in a 10.0 L tank at 27 °C?

  1. Convert to Kelvin: $T = 27 + 273 = 300\text{ K}$.
  2. Rearrange by dividing both sides by $V$: $P = \dfrac{nRT}{V}$.
  3. Put in the numbers: $P = \dfrac{(0.500\ \cancel{\text{mol}})\left(0.08206\ \dfrac{\cancel{\text{L}}\text{ atm}}{\cancel{\text{mol}}\ \cancel{\text{K}}}\right)(300\ \cancel{\text{K}})}{10.0\ \cancel{\text{L}}}$.
  4. The answer is $1.23$ atm. Every unit cancels except atm, which is how you know the setup is right.

Worked example: finding moles, with unit conversions

How many moles of gas are in a 2.50 L flask at 745 mmHg and 22 °C?

  1. Pressure to atm: $745\ \cancel{\text{mmHg}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ atm}}{760\ \cancel{\text{mmHg}}} = 0.980\text{ atm}$.
  2. Temperature to Kelvin: $T = 22 + 273 = 295\text{ K}$.
  3. Rearrange by dividing both sides by $RT$: $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT}$.
  4. Put in the numbers: $n = \dfrac{(0.980\ \cancel{\text{atm}})(2.50\ \cancel{\text{L}})}{\left(0.08206\ \dfrac{\cancel{\text{L}}\ \cancel{\text{atm}}}{\text{mol}\ \cancel{\text{K}}}\right)(295\ \cancel{\text{K}})} = $ $0.101$ mol.

Exam trap: three unit mistakes

With $R = 0.08206$, pressure must be in atm, volume must be in liters, and temperature must be in Kelvin. Using mL gives an answer 1000 times off. Using °C can even give a negative volume, which should set off alarm bells.

Before and after: the combined gas law

When the same sample of gas changes, $n$ and $R$ stay fixed. So $PV/T$ must stay fixed too. That gives a shortcut that works when two things change at once.

The combined gas law (fixed amount of gas)

$$\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$$

Worked example: the weather balloon

A weather balloon holds 5.00 L at 1.00 atm and 20 °C. It rises to where the pressure is 0.300 atm and the temperature is $-40$ °C. What is its volume there?

  1. Convert to Kelvin: $T_1 = 20 + 273 = 293\text{ K}$ and $T_2 = -40 + 273 = 233\text{ K}$.
  2. Solve for $V_2$. Multiply both sides by $T_2$, then divide by $P_2$: $V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{P_1}{P_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$.
  3. Put in the numbers: $V_2 = 5.00\text{ L} \times \dfrac{1.00\ \cancel{\text{atm}}}{0.300\ \cancel{\text{atm}}} \times \dfrac{233\ \cancel{\text{K}}}{293\ \cancel{\text{K}}}$.
  4. The answer is $13.3$ L. Lower pressure makes it grow, cooling makes it shrink a bit, and pressure wins.

STP and the molar volume

Chemists agreed on a reference condition called standard temperature and pressure, or STP. In most general chemistry courses that means 0 °C and 1 atm. Here is the surprise: at STP, one mole of any gas fills the same space.

Exam trap: 22.4 L is only for STP

Use 22.4 L/mol only at 0 °C and 1 atm, and only for gases. At room temperature, or for liquid water, it is simply wrong. When in doubt, use $PV = nRT$, which always works. The newer IUPAC standard uses 1 bar, which gives 22.7 L/mol. Most exams still use 22.4, but follow your own course.

Gas density and molar mass

Why does a helium balloon float while a carbon dioxide balloon sinks? Because at the same temperature and pressure, every gas packs the same number of particles into each liter. So a heavier particle means a denser gas.

Start with $PV = nRT$. Moles are mass over molar mass, so swap in $n = m/M$, then gather $m/V$ on one side. That is density.

Gas density and molar mass

$$PV = \frac{m}{M}RT \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{PM}{RT} \qquad M = \frac{dRT}{P}$$

Worked example: density of a gas

What is the density of $\ce{O2}$ at 25 °C and 1.00 atm?

  1. Convert to Kelvin: $T = 25 + 273 = 298\text{ K}$. Molar mass of $\ce{O2}$ is $2(16.00) = 32.00$ g/mol.
  2. Use $d = \dfrac{PM}{RT}$.
  3. Put in the numbers: $d = \dfrac{(1.00\ \cancel{\text{atm}})(32.00\text{ g}/\cancel{\text{mol}})}{\left(0.08206\ \dfrac{\text{L}\ \cancel{\text{atm}}}{\cancel{\text{mol}}\ \cancel{\text{K}}}\right)(298\ \cancel{\text{K}})} = $ $1.31$ g/L.

Worked example: naming a mystery gas

A gas has a density of 1.96 g/L at STP. What is its molar mass? Which gas could it be?

  1. Use $M = \dfrac{dRT}{P}$ with STP values: $T = 273\text{ K}$ and $P = 1.00$ atm.
  2. Put in the numbers: $M = \dfrac{(1.96\text{ g/L})(0.08206\text{ L atm/mol K})(273\text{ K})}{1.00\text{ atm}} = $ $43.9$ g/mol.
  3. That is very close to $\ce{CO2}$, at 44.01 g/mol.

Gas stoichiometry: the airbag

Reaction problems work exactly as before. The mole is still the bridge. The only new step is that $PV = nRT$ lets you cross between liters of gas and moles of gas.

The recipe never changes. Gas data to moles with $PV = nRT$. Moles to moles with the balanced equation. Moles to whatever you need. I keep it on my burrow wall.

Worked example: gas made from a solid

Heating 10.0 g of $\ce{CaCO3}$ gives $\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$. What volume of $\ce{CO2}$ forms at STP?

  1. Grams to moles: $10.0\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{100.09\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 0.0999\text{ mol CaCO}_3$.
  2. Mole ratio from the equation, 1 to 1: $0.0999\text{ mol CaCO}_3 \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol CO}_2}{1\text{ mol CaCO}_3} = 0.0999\text{ mol CO}_2$.
  3. Moles to liters at STP: $0.0999\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{22.4\text{ L}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = $ $2.24$ L.

Mixtures: Dalton's law of partial pressures

Air is a mix of gases. How do they share the pressure? In an ideal gas, the particles do not care who else is in the room. Each gas pushes on the walls as if it were there alone.

Dalton's law and mole fractions

$$P_\text{total} = P_1 + P_2 + P_3 + \dots \qquad X_i = \frac{n_i}{n_\text{total}} \qquad P_i = X_i\, P_\text{total}$$

Worked example: partial pressure from a mole fraction

A tank holds 2.00 mol of $\ce{N2}$ and 0.500 mol of $\ce{O2}$ at a total pressure of 5.00 atm. What is the partial pressure of $\ce{O2}$?

  1. Total moles: $2.00 + 0.500 = 2.50$ mol.
  2. Mole fraction of oxygen: $X_{\ce{O2}} = \dfrac{0.500\ \cancel{\text{mol}}}{2.50\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = 0.200$. It has no unit.
  3. Partial pressure: $P_{\ce{O2}} = (0.200)(5.00\text{ atm}) = $ $1.00$ atm.

Collecting a gas over water

A classic lab method: bubble a gas into an upside-down tube of water, and read the volume off the side. There is a catch that shows up on nearly every exam. Water evaporates, so the gas you collect is never pure.

Gas collected over water is always a mixture. Subtract the vapor pressure of water before you use $PV = nRT$.

Worked example: hydrogen over water

Hydrogen collected over water at 25 °C fills 125 mL when the air pressure is 757 torr. How many moles of $\ce{H2}$ were collected?

  1. Remove the water vapor. At 25 °C it is 23.8 torr: $P_{\ce{H2}} = 757 - 23.8 = 733.2\text{ torr}$.
  2. Pressure to atm: $733.2\ \cancel{\text{torr}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ atm}}{760\ \cancel{\text{torr}}} = 0.9647\text{ atm}$.
  3. Volume to liters and temperature to Kelvin: $V = 0.125\text{ L}$ and $T = 298\text{ K}$.
  4. Use $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT}$: $n = \dfrac{(0.9647\ \cancel{\text{atm}})(0.125\ \cancel{\text{L}})}{\left(0.08206\ \dfrac{\cancel{\text{L}}\ \cancel{\text{atm}}}{\text{mol}\ \cancel{\text{K}}}\right)(298\ \cancel{\text{K}})} = $ $4.93 \times 10^{-3}$ mol.

Pocket card

  • $PV = nRT$, with $R = 0.08206$ L atm/(mol K). Use atm, L, mol and K. The other value, $8.314$ J/(mol K), goes with energy and SI units.
  • Same gas changing: $\dfrac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$.
  • STP is 0 °C and 1 atm, where 1 mol of gas fills 22.4 L. With 1 bar it is 22.7 L.
  • Density: $d = \dfrac{PM}{RT}$. Molar mass: $M = \dfrac{dRT}{P}$.
  • Mixtures: $P_\text{total} = P_1 + P_2 + \dots$ and $P_i = X_i P_\text{total}$.
  • Over water: $P_\text{gas} = P_\text{atm} - P_{\ce{H2O}}$.
