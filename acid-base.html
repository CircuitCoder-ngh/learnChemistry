Acids, bases and titration

An acid hands over a proton and a base takes it. Count those protons, and one drop of color can reveal a concentration you never measured.

  • Define acids and bases, both Arrhenius and Brønsted-Lowry
  • Name the six strong acids and the strong bases
  • Write neutralization equations, down to the net ionic
  • Find an unknown concentration from titration data

An acid-base reaction passes a proton

A hydrogen atom is one proton and one electron. Take the electron away and only the proton is left, which is why chemists use $\ce{H+}$ and "proton" as the same word. Acids and bases are all about where that proton goes.

An acid gives a proton. A base takes a proton. Every acid-base reaction is one proton changing hands.

Two definitions you need

Arrhenius
An acid makes $\ce{H+}$ in water. A base makes $\ce{OH-}$ in water.
Brønsted-Lowry
An acid donates a proton. A base accepts a proton. This one is broader, and it covers $\ce{NH3}$.

Try it with your own hands. The cyan dots on the base are a lone pair: two electrons ready to hold a proton. No lone pair, no catch.

Exam trap: conjugate pairs differ by exactly one proton

The conjugate base of $\ce{H2SO4}$ is $\ce{HSO4-}$, not $\ce{SO4^2-}$. Take away one $\ce{H+}$: remove one H and drop the charge by 1. To get a conjugate acid, add one H and raise the charge by 1.

Strong and weak

A strong acid gives up its proton in water. Every molecule splits. (For $\ce{H2SO4}$, that means the first proton.) A weak acid mostly hangs on, so only a small fraction splits at any moment. You must memorize the short list of strong ones. Assume any other acid is weak.

Exam trap: strong does not mean concentrated

Strong and weak tell you what fraction splits into ions. Concentrated and dilute tell you how much is dissolved. Vinegar can be concentrated and still weak. A tiny amount of HCl in a lake is dilute and still strong.

Neutralization: acid plus base

When an acid meets a hydroxide base, the protons and hydroxide ions snap together into water. What remains is an ionic compound called a salt. Table salt is just one example.

Neutralization

$$\text{acid} + \text{base} \rightarrow \text{salt} + \text{water}$$ $$\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}$$
Note
That net ionic equation holds for any strong acid with any strong base, as long as the salt stays dissolved.

Count the protons before you balance. $\ce{H2SO4}$ can give two, so it takes two $\ce{NaOH}$. $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ brings two $\ce{OH-}$, so it takes two $\ce{HCl}$. My whole job is counting, and so is yours.

Titration: counting with a color change

Suppose you have an acid but not its concentration. Add a base of known concentration, one drop at a time, until the acid is exactly used up. The point where moles of $\ce{H+}$ equal moles of $\ce{OH-}$ is the equivalence point.

You cannot see that point by itself, because both solutions are clear. So you add a little phenolphthalein, an indicator that is colorless in acid and pink in base. One drop past the equivalence point, the flask turns pink. That color change is the end point.

At the equivalence point

$$\text{moles of } \ce{H+} = \text{moles of } \ce{OH-}$$
Moles
molarity × volume in liters
Ratio
Use the balanced equation. It is not always 1 to 1.

Worked example: a 1 to 1 titration

It takes 31.40 mL of 0.1000 M $\ce{NaOH}$ to reach the end point with 25.00 mL of $\ce{HCl}$. What is the molarity of the $\ce{HCl}$?

  1. Write the equation: $\ce{HCl(aq)}$ + $\ce{NaOH(aq)}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{NaCl(aq)}$ + $\ce{H2O(l)}$. The ratio is 1 to 1.
  2. Moles of $\ce{NaOH}$: $0.03140\ \cancel{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{0.1000\text{ mol}}{1\ \cancel{\text{L}}} = 3.140 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$.
  3. Moles of $\ce{HCl}$: $3.140 \times 10^{-3}\ \cancel{\text{mol NaOH}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol HCl}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol NaOH}}} = 3.140 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$.
  4. Molarity of $\ce{HCl}$: $\dfrac{3.140 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}}{0.02500\text{ L}} =$ 0.1256 M.

Worked example: an acid with two protons

It takes 28.40 mL of 0.1500 M $\ce{NaOH}$ to reach the end point with 20.00 mL of $\ce{H2SO4}$. What is the molarity of the $\ce{H2SO4}$?

  1. Write the equation: $\ce{H2SO4(aq)}$ + $\ce{2NaOH(aq)}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{Na2SO4(aq)}$ + $\ce{2H2O(l)}$. The ratio is 1 to 2.
  2. Moles of $\ce{NaOH}$: $0.02840\text{ L} \times 0.1500\text{ mol/L} = 4.260 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$.
  3. Moles of $\ce{H2SO4}$: $4.260 \times 10^{-3}\ \cancel{\text{mol NaOH}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol H}_2\text{SO}_4}{2\ \cancel{\text{mol NaOH}}} = 2.130 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$.
  4. Molarity of $\ce{H2SO4}$: $\dfrac{2.130 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}}{0.02000\text{ L}} =$ 0.1065 M.

Exam trap: $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ only works for 1 to 1

That shortcut is for dilution and for 1 to 1 reactions. Use it on $\ce{H2SO4}$ with $\ce{NaOH}$ and your answer is off by a factor of 2. In a titration, always go through moles and the balanced equation.

Pocket card

  • Brønsted-Lowry: acids donate $\ce{H+}$, bases accept $\ce{H+}$. Arrhenius: acids make $\ce{H+}$ in water, bases make $\ce{OH-}$.
  • Strong acids: $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HBr}$, $\ce{HI}$, $\ce{HNO3}$, $\ce{H2SO4}$, $\ce{HClO4}$. Strong bases: group 1 hydroxides, and $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, $\ce{Sr(OH)2}$, $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$.
  • Neutralization: acid + base gives salt + water. Net ionic for strong acid and strong base: $\ce{H+ + OH- -> H2O}$.
  • Equivalence point: moles of $\ce{H+}$ equal moles of $\ce{OH-}$. End point: the indicator changes color.
  • Titration path: volume and molarity of base, to moles of base, to moles of acid through the equation, to molarity of acid.
