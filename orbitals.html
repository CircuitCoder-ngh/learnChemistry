Orbitals and quantum numbers

An electron has no neat path. It has a cloud of places it might be, and four numbers that work like its home address.

  • Say what an orbital is, and how it differs from an orbit
  • Use $n$, $\ell$, $m_\ell$ and $m_s$ as an address, and spot illegal ones
  • Recognize s, p and d shapes, and count their nodes
  • Count the orbitals and electrons in any subshell or shell

From orbit to cloud

The last lesson ended with a problem. You cannot track an electron along a path. So what can you say about where it is? Quite a lot, it turns out, as long as you think in odds.

An orbital is not a path. It is a region of space, usually drawn to hold 90 percent of the chance of finding the electron.

Exam trap: orbit is not orbital

An orbit is Bohr's sharp circular track, and we no longer believe in it. An orbital is a wave-based cloud of probability. The electron does not run along the surface of a balloon. It has some chance of being anywhere inside it, and a small chance of being outside.

The orbital viewer

These are real hydrogen wavefunctions, solved by Schrödinger's equation and plotted in 3D. In cloud mode, each dot is one place the electron could be found. In balloon mode, you see the simple outline that textbooks draw. Pick the numbers and turn it with your finger.

Cyan and pink mark the phase of the wave, like the up and down of a wave on a rope. They are not charges, and pink is not "less electron". Try 2p, then press All of this subshell in cloud mode. Together, the three p orbitals make a perfect sphere. I did not expect that either.

Four numbers, one address

Every electron in an atom has its own address, made of four quantum numbers. Think of a city of tall buildings: the number tells you the building, the floor, the room, and the seat.

The rules for the address

$$n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$$
$$\ell = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1$$
$$m_\ell = -\ell, \ldots, 0, \ldots, +\ell$$
$$m_s = +\tfrac{1}{2} \text{ or } -\tfrac{1}{2}$$
$\ell$ names
$\ell = 0$ is s, $\ell = 1$ is p, $\ell = 2$ is d, $\ell = 3$ is f
Counts
A subshell has $2\ell + 1$ orbitals. Shell $n$ has $n^2$ orbitals and holds $2n^2$ electrons.

Worked example: is this address real?

Which of these sets is not allowed, and why? (a) $n=3, \ell=2, m_\ell=-1, m_s=+\tfrac{1}{2}$   (b) $n=2, \ell=2, m_\ell=0, m_s=-\tfrac{1}{2}$   (c) $n=4, \ell=1, m_\ell=2, m_s=+\tfrac{1}{2}$

  1. Check (a). $\ell$ must be 0, 1 or 2 when $n=3$, so 2 is fine. $m_\ell$ runs from $-2$ to $+2$, so $-1$ is fine. Allowed. It is a 3d electron.
  2. Check (b). When $n=2$, $\ell$ can only be 0 or 1. $\ell=2$ breaks the rule, so a "2d" orbital does not exist.
  3. Check (c). $\ell=1$ is fine for $n=4$, but $m_\ell$ may only be $-1$, 0 or $+1$. $m_\ell=2$ breaks the rule.
  4. So (b) and (c) are not allowed.

Worked example: counting orbitals and electrons

How many orbitals and electrons fit in the $n = 3$ shell? How many in just the 3d subshell?

  1. Shell: orbitals $= n^2 = 3^2 = 9$.
  2. Each orbital holds 2 electrons, so $2n^2 = 2(9) = 18$ electrons.
  3. Check by adding subshells: 3s has 1, 3p has 3, 3d has 5, and $1 + 3 + 5 = 9$ orbitals.
  4. Subshell 3d: $\ell = 2$, so $2\ell + 1 = 5$ orbitals, which hold 10 electrons. The whole shell holds 18.

Exam trap: $\ell$ stops one short of $n$

The largest $\ell$ is $n - 1$, not $n$. So there is no 1p, no 2d and no 3f. If you see any of those in a question, the answer is "does not exist". This is the single most tested rule in this topic.

Nodes: where the electron never is

Because electrons act like waves, orbitals have nodes: places where the wave is exactly zero, so the chance of finding the electron there is zero too. Nodes follow a simple counting rule.

Counting nodes

$$\text{total nodes} = n - 1$$
$$\text{angular} = \ell \qquad \text{radial} = n - \ell - 1$$

Worked example: nodes in a 4d orbital

How many nodes of each kind does a 4d orbital have?

  1. Read the name: 4d means $n = 4$ and $\ell = 2$.
  2. Total: $n - 1 = 4 - 1 = 3$ nodes.
  3. Angular: $\ell = 2$ nodes. Radial: $n - \ell - 1 = 4 - 2 - 1 = 1$ node.
  4. Check: $2 + 1 = 3$. The answer is 2 angular and 1 radial.

People always ask how the electron crosses a node if it can never be there. It does not cross like a ball. It is a wave, spread out on both sides at once, the way a guitar string vibrates on both sides of a still point.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Orbital: a region with about 90% chance of finding the electron. Not a path.
  • $n$ sets size and energy, and is 1 or higher. $\ell$ sets shape, from 0 to $n - 1$. $m_\ell$ sets direction, from $-\ell$ to $+\ell$. $m_s$ is $+\tfrac{1}{2}$ or $-\tfrac{1}{2}$.
  • $\ell$ = 0, 1, 2, 3 means s, p, d, f. There is no 1p, 2d or 3f.
  • A subshell has $2\ell + 1$ orbitals: s has 1, p has 3, d has 5, f has 7.
  • A shell has $n^2$ orbitals and holds $2n^2$ electrons. Each orbital holds 2.
  • Nodes: total $n - 1$, angular $\ell$, radial $n - \ell - 1$.
