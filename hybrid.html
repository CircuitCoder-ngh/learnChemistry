Hybrid orbitals and MO theory

Bonds are overlapping electron waves. Two models show how: one mixes orbitals on an atom, and the other shares them across the whole molecule.

  • Find the bond order and predict if a molecule is magnetic

Sigma and pi: two ways to overlap

Orbitals are the regions where electrons live, as you saw in the orbitals lesson. A bond forms when an orbital on one atom overlaps an orbital on another. How they overlap matters. This is valence bond theory.

The first bond between two atoms is always sigma. Every extra bond is pi. A double bond is one of each. A triple bond is one sigma and two pi.

Exam trap: counting sigma and pi

Count one sigma for every connection between two atoms, including all the C-H bonds. Then add pi only for extras. Ethene has 5 sigma bonds and 1 pi bond, not 1 sigma and 1 pi. Students often forget the C-H bonds.

Hybrid orbitals

Here is a puzzle. A carbon atom has one round 2s orbital and three 2p orbitals at 90° to each other. So how can methane have four identical bonds pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron? Atoms solve this by mixing their orbitals.

Count sigma, count pi

Tap each bond in the molecule to split it into its sigma and pi parts. Tap each atom to see how it is hybridized. Lone pairs count as groups too.

Worked example: a real molecule

For acetonitrile, $\ce{CH3-C#N}$, count sigma and pi bonds and find the hybridization of each carbon.

  1. Sigma: 3 C-H bonds + 1 C-C bond + 1 C-N bond = 5 sigma.
  2. Pi: the triple bond has 3 pairs, so subtract the sigma: $3 - 1 = 2$. That gives 2 pi.
  3. First carbon: 4 groups (3 H and 1 C), so it is $sp^3$.
  4. Second carbon: 2 groups (1 C and the triple bond, counted once), so it is $sp$.
  5. Answer: 5 sigma, 2 pi, with carbons $sp^3$ and $sp$.

When Lewis structures fail

Valence bond theory works beautifully for shapes. But there is one famous failure, and you can see it with a magnet. To explain it, you need molecular orbital theory, where electrons belong to the whole molecule.

Bond order in MO theory

$$\text{bond order} = \frac{\text{bonding electrons} - \text{antibonding electrons}}{2}$$

Fill an MO diagram

The rules are the same as for an atom. Fill the lowest level first, put at most 2 electrons in a box, and spread out singly among equal boxes before pairing (Hund's rule). Pick a molecule and tap the boxes, or let the auto-fill do it.

Worked example: oxygen

Use MO theory to find the bond order and magnetism of $\ce{O2}$.

  1. Valence electrons: $2 \times 6 = 12$.
  2. Oxygen uses the order with $\sigma_{2p}$ below $\pi_{2p}$. Fill: $(\sigma_{2s})^2\ (\sigma^*_{2s})^2\ (\sigma_{2p})^2\ (\pi_{2p})^4\ (\pi^*_{2p})^2$.
  3. Bonding: $2 + 2 + 4 = 8$. Antibonding: $2 + 2 = 4$.
  4. Bond order $= \dfrac{8 - 4}{2} = 2$. This agrees with the double bond in the Lewis structure.
  5. The two $\pi^*$ electrons sit in separate boxes (Hund's rule): bond order 2, paramagnetic with 2 unpaired electrons.

Exam trap: the switch and the ions

Use the order with $\pi_{2p}$ below $\sigma_{2p}$ for everything up to $\ce{N2}$, and $\sigma_{2p}$ below $\pi_{2p}$ from $\ce{O2}$ on. For ions, use the order of the neutral element. Taking an electron from an antibonding level, as in $\ce{O2+}$, raises the bond order. Taking one from a bonding level, as in $\ce{N2+}$, lowers it.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Sigma is head-on overlap on the axis. Pi is side-on overlap above and below it, and blocks rotation.
  • Single: 1 sigma. Double: 1 sigma and 1 pi. Triple: 1 sigma and 2 pi.
  • 2 groups $sp$, 3 groups $sp^2$, 4 groups $sp^3$. Leftover p orbitals make pi bonds.
  • Bonding MOs are lower, antibonding MOs are higher. Bond order $= (\text{bonding} - \text{antibonding}) / 2$.
  • Order switch: $\pi_{2p}$ is below $\sigma_{2p}$ through $\ce{N2}$, then above from $\ce{O2}$ on.
  • Unpaired electrons mean paramagnetic. $\ce{O2}$ has 2 of them, in its $\pi^*$ orbitals.
