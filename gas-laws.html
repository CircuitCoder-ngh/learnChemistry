Pressure and the gas laws

Gas is a crowd of tiny things crashing into the walls. Squeeze it, heat it or add more, and you can predict exactly what happens.

  • Explain pressure as particles hitting a wall
  • Convert between atm, mmHg, torr and kPa
  • Use Boyle's, Charles's, Gay-Lussac's and Avogadro's laws
  • Always switch to Kelvin, and say why Celsius fails

What is pressure?

A blown-up balloon is hollow, yet it stays round. Something inside is pushing on the rubber, all the time, in every direction. Step through the story to find out what.

Pressure is force spread over an area. In a gas, that force comes from countless particles hitting the walls.

Pressure

$$P = \frac{F}{A} \qquad 1\text{ Pa} = 1\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^2}$$

Weighing the air with a barometer

In 1643 Evangelista Torricelli found a way to measure the push of the air using nothing but a tube and a dish of mercury. His device is the barometer, and it gave us a unit we still use.

Four names for the same push

Climb a mountain and there is less air above you, so it pushes down less. The mercury drops. Slide the altitude and watch all the units change together.

Pressure units: all the same push

$$\begin{gathered}1\text{ atm} = 760\text{ mmHg} = 760\text{ torr}\\ = 101.325\text{ kPa} = 1.01325\text{ bar}\end{gathered}$$

Worked example: converting pressure

A barometer reads 745 mmHg. What is that in atm and in kPa?

  1. Write the fraction so mmHg cancels: $745\ \cancel{\text{mmHg}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ atm}}{760\ \cancel{\text{mmHg}}} = 0.980\text{ atm}$.
  2. Carry on from atm to kPa: $0.980\ \cancel{\text{atm}} \times \dfrac{101.325\text{ kPa}}{1\ \cancel{\text{atm}}} = 99.3\text{ kPa}$.
  3. The answer is $0.980$ atm, or $99.3$ kPa. Three significant figures, because 745 has three. The 760 and 101.325 are exact definitions.

Why the gas laws happen

A gas has four things you can measure: pressure $P$, volume $V$, temperature $T$ and amount $n$ in moles. Hold two steady and the other two move together. Each pair has a law named after the person who pinned it down.

LawHeld steadyWhat happensEquation
Boyle$T$, $n$Less volume, more pressure$P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$
Charles$P$, $n$Hotter, more volume$\dfrac{V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2}$
Gay-Lussac$V$, $n$Hotter, more pressure$\dfrac{P_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2}{T_2}$
Avogadro$P$, $T$More gas, more volume$\dfrac{V_1}{n_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{n_2}$

Try the graph button on Boyle's law. $P$ against $V$ is a curve, which is hard to read. $P$ against $1/V$ is a straight line through zero, and straight lines are how scientists prove a law.

Worked example: Boyle's law

A gas fills 5.00 L at 760 mmHg. At the same temperature, what volume does it fill at 2.50 atm?

  1. Check what is steady: $T$ and $n$. That is Boyle's law, $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$.
  2. Make the pressure units match: $760\ \cancel{\text{mmHg}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ atm}}{760\ \cancel{\text{mmHg}}} = 1.00\text{ atm}$.
  3. Solve for $V_2$ by dividing both sides by $P_2$: $V_2 = \dfrac{P_1V_1}{P_2}$.
  4. Put in the numbers: $V_2 = \dfrac{(1.00\ \cancel{\text{atm}})(5.00\text{ L})}{2.50\ \cancel{\text{atm}}} = $ $2.00$ L.
  5. Sense check: pressure went up, so the volume should go down. It did.

Why Celsius breaks the gas laws

This is the most common mistake in the whole unit. Charles's law and Gay-Lussac's law say a gas scales with temperature. But only one temperature scale starts at a real zero.

Exam trap: Celsius in a gas law

A gas heated from 25 °C to 50 °C does not double in volume. In Kelvin it goes from 298 K to 323 K, only 8.4% more. Convert first, every single time: $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$. Many courses round this to 273.

Worked example: Charles's law, with the Celsius trap

A balloon holds 2.00 L at 25 °C. It is warmed to 100 °C at constant pressure. What is its new volume?

  1. Convert to Kelvin first: $T_1 = 25 + 273 = 298\text{ K}$ and $T_2 = 100 + 273 = 373\text{ K}$.
  2. Write Charles's law and solve for $V_2$ by multiplying both sides by $T_2$: $\dfrac{V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2}$, so $V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$.
  3. Put in the numbers: $V_2 = 2.00\text{ L} \times \dfrac{373\ \cancel{\text{K}}}{298\ \cancel{\text{K}}} = $ $2.50$ L.
  4. The Celsius mistake would give $2.00 \times \frac{100}{25} = 8.00$ L, which is more than three times too big.

Worked example: Gay-Lussac's law

A sealed spray can holds gas at 1.20 atm and 22 °C. It lands in a 400 °C campfire. What does the pressure reach before it bursts?

  1. The can is rigid, so $V$ and $n$ are steady. That is Gay-Lussac's law.
  2. Convert: $T_1 = 22 + 273 = 295\text{ K}$ and $T_2 = 400 + 273 = 673\text{ K}$.
  3. Solve for $P_2$: $P_2 = P_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$.
  4. Put in the numbers: $P_2 = 1.20\text{ atm} \times \dfrac{673\ \cancel{\text{K}}}{295\ \cancel{\text{K}}} = $ $2.74$ atm. This is why spray cans warn you never to burn them.

Worked example: Avogadro's law

A balloon holds 0.500 mol of helium in 12.0 L. How large is it with 0.750 mol at the same temperature and pressure?

  1. $P$ and $T$ are steady, so use Avogadro's law: $\dfrac{V_1}{n_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{n_2}$.
  2. Solve for $V_2$: $V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$.
  3. Put in the numbers: $V_2 = 12.0\text{ L} \times \dfrac{0.750\ \cancel{\text{mol}}}{0.500\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = $ $18.0$ L.

Exam trap: mixed pressure units

Boyle's law works in any pressure unit, as long as both pressures use the same one. If $P_1$ is in mmHg and $P_2$ is in atm, the answer will be off by a factor of 760. Convert before you calculate.

Pocket card

  • Pressure is force per area. In a gas it comes from particles hitting the walls.
  • $1\text{ atm} = 760\text{ mmHg} = 760\text{ torr} = 101.325\text{ kPa}$.
  • Boyle: $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$. Charles: $V_1/T_1 = V_2/T_2$.
  • Gay-Lussac: $P_1/T_1 = P_2/T_2$. Avogadro: $V_1/n_1 = V_2/n_2$.
  • Temperature must be in Kelvin. Add 273.15 to Celsius. No exceptions.
