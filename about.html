What is on this site, why it is there, and where the numbers come from.

What is covered, and why

The lessons follow the order of OpenStax Chemistry 2e, a free textbook many first-year courses use. Chapters 1 to 11 are the core. Chapters 12 to 17, 20 and 21 are shorter next-term lessons, since some Chem 101 courses get that far.

To check nothing is missing, we compared the list against the ACS General Chemistry First Term exam topics:

Combustion equations and solubility rules each get a full lesson by request. The most animation went to the mole and stoichiometry, because Ralph and Lewis (2018) found at-risk students struggle most with exactly those topics.

How it teaches

The design comes from chemistry education research. Johnstone's three views link what you see, the particles, and the symbols. Animations advance when you press Next, following Mayer's work on segmenting and signaling. Every lesson gives you something to change, as PhET's simulations do, and ends with questions that make you produce the answer.

Data

Element data comes from the PubChem periodic table, which is public domain, with atomic masses set to the IUPAC abridged values textbooks print. Covalent radii are from Cordero et al., Dalton Transactions, 2008. PubChem rounds some atomic masses, so the last digit can differ from your textbook's table. We corrected a few discovery years and one set of oxidation states on the periodic table page, where they conflicted with the standard account.

Libraries and type

KaTeX sets the math and its mhchem extension sets the chemistry. three.js draws the 3D models. Headlines are in Lilita One, and body text is in Atkinson Hyperlegible Next, chosen because it keeps 1, l and I, and 0 and O, clearly apart.

Please read this

This is a study aid, not a course. If your instructor's rules, tables or definitions differ from what you see here, your instructor's rules win. The models here are simplified on purpose, and they say so where it matters.
