Kinetic theory and real gases

Why do all gases follow the same simple law? Because a gas is mostly empty space with tiny things flying through it, until you crowd them.

  • Use Graham's law and explain when real gases stop being ideal

The five ideas of kinetic theory

Kinetic molecular theory, or KMT, is a simple picture of what a gas is. Five ideas, called postulates, are enough to explain every gas law from the last two lessons.

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles. Hotter means faster, on average.

Average kinetic energy of a mole of gas particles

$$\text{KE}_\text{avg} = \frac{3}{2}RT \qquad R = 8.314\ \frac{\text{J}}{\text{mol K}}$$

Not everyone moves at the same speed

Temperature sets the average. Each particle has its own speed, and every collision changes it. Start them all at one speed below and watch what collisions do. The bars count the real particles in the box.

Notice the long tail on the right. A few particles are always far faster than average. That tail is why water can evaporate from a puddle long before it reaches 100 °C.

How fast? The rms speed

The real 3D curve is called the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. To sum up all those speeds in one number, we use the root mean square speed: square each speed, average them, and take the square root. Same temperature means same kinetic energy, so lighter particles must move faster.

Root mean square speed

$$u_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}} \qquad R = 8.314\ \frac{\text{J}}{\text{mol K}}, \quad M \text{ in kg/mol}$$

Worked example: rms speed

What is the rms speed of $\ce{N2}$ molecules at 25 °C?

  1. Temperature to Kelvin: $T = 25 + 273 = 298\text{ K}$.
  2. Molar mass to kg/mol: $28.02\ \cancel{\text{g}}/\text{mol} \times \dfrac{1\text{ kg}}{1000\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 0.02802\text{ kg/mol}$.
  3. Use the joule trick. $1\text{ J} = 1\text{ kg m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so that is the unit of $RT$ per mole.
  4. Put in the numbers: $u_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\dfrac{3\left(8.314\ \dfrac{\cancel{\text{kg}}\text{ m}^2}{\text{s}^2\ \cancel{\text{mol}}\ \cancel{\text{K}}}\right)(298\ \cancel{\text{K}})}{0.02802\ \cancel{\text{kg}}/\cancel{\text{mol}}}} = \sqrt{2.65 \times 10^5\ \dfrac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}}$.
  5. The answer is $515$ m/s, over 1000 miles per hour. Molecules in your room are doing this right now.

Exam trap: molar mass in kg/mol

With $R = 8.314$, the molar mass must be in kilograms per mole. Plug in 28.02 instead of 0.02802 and $\ce{N2}$ crawls along at 16 m/s, slower than a car on a highway. A second trap: quadruple the Kelvin temperature and speed only doubles, because of the square root.

Graham's law: the effusion race

Effusion is gas escaping through a tiny hole into empty space. A faster particle reaches the hole more often, so lighter gases leak out faster. Thomas Graham measured this in the 1840s.

Graham's law of effusion

$$\frac{\text{rate}_1}{\text{rate}_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}}$$

Worked example: comparing rates

How many times faster does $\ce{H2}$ effuse than $\ce{O2}$ at the same temperature?

  1. Call hydrogen gas 1 and oxygen gas 2: $M_1 = 2.016$ g/mol and $M_2 = 32.00$ g/mol.
  2. Note that the other gas's mass goes on top: $\dfrac{\text{rate}_{\ce{H2}}}{\text{rate}_{\ce{O2}}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{M_{\ce{O2}}}{M_{\ce{H2}}}}$.
  3. Put in the numbers: $\sqrt{\dfrac{32.00\ \cancel{\text{g/mol}}}{2.016\ \cancel{\text{g/mol}}}} = \sqrt{15.87} = $ $3.98$ times faster.

Worked example: identifying a mystery gas

An unknown gas effuses at 0.500 times the rate of helium. What is its molar mass?

  1. Write Graham's law with the unknown as gas $x$: $\dfrac{\text{rate}_x}{\text{rate}_{\ce{He}}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{M_{\ce{He}}}{M_x}}$, so $0.500 = \sqrt{\dfrac{4.003}{M_x}}$.
  2. Square both sides to remove the root: $0.250 = \dfrac{4.003}{M_x}$.
  3. Solve for $M_x$: $M_x = \dfrac{4.003\text{ g/mol}}{0.250} = $ $16.0$ g/mol. That matches methane, $\ce{CH4}$.

Diffusion: spreading through a crowd

Diffusion is one gas spreading through another. It follows the same rule, lighter is faster. But it is far slower than the molecular speeds suggest, and the story shows why.

Exam trap: effusion is not diffusion

Effusion means escaping through a tiny hole into a vacuum, with no collisions in the way. Diffusion means mixing through another gas, with constant collisions. Graham's law is exact for effusion and only a rough guide for diffusion.

Real gases: when the ideal breaks

Two of the five postulates are not quite true. Molecules do take up space, and they do attract each other. Most of the time it barely matters. Crowd and chill a gas, and it matters a lot.

The van der Waals equation

$$\left(P + a\frac{n^2}{V^2}\right)\left(V - nb\right) = nRT$$
Gas$a$ (L² atm/mol²)$b$ (L/mol)What it says
$\ce{He}$0.03410.0237Tiny, barely sticky
$\ce{H2}$0.2440.0266Small, nonpolar
$\ce{N2}$1.390.0391Larger, a little sticky
$\ce{CO2}$3.590.0427Larger, stickier
$\ce{NH3}$4.170.0371Polar and hydrogen bonds, very sticky

Worked example: ideal versus real

Find the pressure of 1.00 mol of $\ce{CO2}$ in 1.00 L at 300 K, first as an ideal gas, then with van der Waals. Use $a = 3.59$ and $b = 0.0427$.

  1. Ideal: $P = \dfrac{nRT}{V} = \dfrac{(1.00)(0.08206)(300)}{1.00} = 24.6\text{ atm}$.
  2. Solve van der Waals for $P$: subtract the attraction term, $P = \dfrac{nRT}{V - nb} - a\dfrac{n^2}{V^2}$.
  3. The size term: $\dfrac{(1.00)(0.08206)(300)}{1.00 - (1.00)(0.0427)} = \dfrac{24.62}{0.9573} = 25.72\text{ atm}$.
  4. The attraction term: $3.59 \times \dfrac{(1.00)^2}{(1.00)^2} = 3.59\text{ atm}$.
  5. Together: $P = 25.72 - 3.59 = $ $22.1$ atm. That is below ideal, because at this density attractions matter more than size.

Exam trap: which conditions are most ideal?

A gas is most ideal at low pressure and high temperature: spread out, so size does not matter, and fast, so attractions do not matter. Students often flip this. The most ideal gases are small and nonpolar, such as helium. Real gases fail at high pressure and low temperature.

Pocket card

  • KMT: tiny particles, straight-line random motion, no forces between them, elastic collisions, average KE set by Kelvin temperature.
  • $\text{KE}_\text{avg} = \frac{3}{2}RT$ per mole. Same $T$ means same KE for any gas.
  • $u_\text{rms} = \sqrt{3RT/M}$, with $R = 8.314$ and $M$ in kg/mol.
  • Graham: $\text{rate}_1/\text{rate}_2 = \sqrt{M_2/M_1}$. Lighter means faster.
  • Van der Waals: $a$ corrects for attractions, $b$ for molecular size.
  • Most ideal at low $P$ and high $T$. $Z = PV/nRT$ is 1 for an ideal gas.
