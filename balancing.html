Balancing equations

Atoms are never lost, so both sides of an equation must match. Here is how to make them match, every time.

  • Explain why an equation has to balance
  • Count the atoms in something like $\ce{3Ca(NO3)2}$
  • Say why you may change coefficients but never subscripts
  • Balance any equation with one reliable method

Why equations must balance

An equation is a record of what happened to some atoms. If the record says an atom vanished, the record is wrong. Watch what goes wrong when you write hydrogen burning the quick way.

Atoms are not created or destroyed in a reaction. They only change partners. So every atom on the left must appear on the right.

Reading a coefficient

$$\underbrace{3}_{\text{coefficient}}\ce{Ca(NO3)2} \;\Rightarrow\; 3\,\text{Ca},\quad 3 \times 2 = 6\,\text{N},\quad 3 \times 2 \times 3 = 18\,\text{O}$$
Coefficient
multiplies everything in the formula that follows it.
Subscript
says how many of the one atom or group it follows.

Exam trap: never touch a subscript

$\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{H2O2}$ are different substances. If you change a subscript, your equation may count correctly, but it describes a reaction that did not happen. To balance, change only the big numbers in front.

The balancer

Your turn. Tap the top half of a coefficient to raise it and the bottom half to lower it. The scale really weighs each side, atom by atom. When every element matches, it settles. There are 18 equations, and they get harder.

A method that always works

Trial and error works on easy equations and burns your exam time on hard ones. This order makes each number forced, so you rarely have to go back.

The balancing order

$\text{biggest formula} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{rare elements} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{polyatomic ions} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{H} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{O}$
Start
Set the most complicated formula to 1.
Units
If $\ce{SO4}$ or $\ce{PO4}$ appears unchanged on both sides, count it as one thing.
Last
Leave H and O for the end. They usually appear in many places.
Fractions
Allowed on the way, then multiply everything to clear them.

Notice the phosphate never got taken apart. Why count 8 oxygens when you can count 2 phosphates? I am lazy, and in chemistry that is called good technique.

Worked example: a fraction on the way

Balance $\ce{Al + O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{Al2O3}$.

  1. Set the biggest formula to 1: one $\ce{Al2O3}$.
  2. Aluminum. The right has 2 Al, so write $\ce{2Al}$ on the left.
  3. Oxygen. The right has 3 O. $\ce{O2}$ comes in pairs, so $3 \div 2 = \tfrac{3}{2}$. That gives $\ce{2Al} + \tfrac{3}{2}\ce{O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{Al2O3}$.
  4. Clear the fraction. Multiply every coefficient by 2: $\ce{4Al + 3O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{2Al2O3}$.
  5. Check. Al: 4 and $2 \times 2 = 4$. O: $3 \times 2 = 6$ and $2 \times 3 = 6$. Balanced.

Worked example: keep the phosphate whole

Balance $\ce{Ca(OH)2 + H3PO4}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{Ca3(PO4)2 + H2O}$.

  1. Set the biggest formula to 1: one $\ce{Ca3(PO4)2}$.
  2. Calcium. The right has 3 Ca, so write $\ce{3Ca(OH)2}$.
  3. Phosphate, as a unit. The right has 2 $\ce{PO4}$, so write $\ce{2H3PO4}$.
  4. Hydrogen. The left has $3 \times 2 + 2 \times 3 = 12$ H, so write $\ce{6H2O}$.
  5. Oxygen. Outside the phosphate, the left has $3 \times 2 = 6$ O and the right has $6 \times 1 = 6$ O. It works.
  6. Answer: $\ce{3Ca(OH)2 + 2H3PO4}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{Ca3(PO4)2 + 6H2O}$.

Exam trap: the smallest whole numbers

$\ce{4H2 + 2O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{4H2O}$ counts correctly, but every number shares a factor of 2. Exams want $\ce{2H2 + O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{2H2O}$. Also remember a coefficient of 1 is left unwritten.

Phase labels

Equations often carry small tags that say what state each substance is in. They do not affect the balancing, but exams expect you to read them. The tag $\text{(aq)}$ stands for aqueous, which means dissolved in water.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Atoms are conserved, so each element must match on both sides.
  • Change coefficients only. Changing a subscript makes a different substance.
  • A coefficient multiplies the whole formula: $\ce{3Ca(NO3)2}$ has 3 Ca, 6 N and 18 O.
  • Order: biggest formula, rare elements, polyatomic ions as units, then H and O last.
  • Clear fractions by multiplying every term. Finish with the smallest whole numbers.
  • Tags: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water.
