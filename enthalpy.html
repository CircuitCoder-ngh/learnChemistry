Enthalpy and Hess's law

You do not have to run a reaction to know its heat. You can add up other reactions like a puzzle, and this page turns that into a game.

  • Read an energy diagram and give $\Delta H$ the right sign
  • Reverse and scale thermochemical equations
  • Solve Hess's law problems by adding reactions
  • Find $\Delta H^\circ$ from formation enthalpies and from bond enthalpies

What is enthalpy?

Think of a reaction as moving between two shelves. Each substance has a height called its enthalpy, $H$. You cannot measure the height itself, but you can measure how far things drop or climb. That change, $\Delta H$, is the heat you measured in a coffee cup. At constant pressure, $\Delta H = q$.

Enthalpy change

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta H = H_{\text{products}} - H_{\text{reactants}} \\ \Delta H = q \text{ at constant pressure}\end{gathered}$$
$\Delta H < 0$
exothermic. Products are lower. Heat leaves the system.
$\Delta H > 0$
endothermic. Products are higher. Heat enters the system.

Exam trap: the sign and the size travel with the equation

$\Delta H$ belongs to the equation exactly as written. Write it backward and the sign flips. Double the coefficients and $\Delta H$ doubles. Change a phase, such as $\ce{H2O(g)}$ to $\ce{H2O(l)}$, and $\Delta H$ changes too.

Worked example: heat from a mass of fuel

$\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) ->}$ $\ce{CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}$ has $\Delta H = -890.3$ kJ. How much heat comes from burning 8.00 g of methane?

  1. Read the equation as a recipe: 1 mol of $\ce{CH4}$ goes with $-890.3$ kJ.
  2. Convert grams to moles: $8.00\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{16.04\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 0.499\text{ mol}$.
  3. Use the heat as a conversion factor: $0.499\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{-890.3\text{ kJ}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = $ $-444$ kJ. The minus sign says that heat is released.

Hess's law: the route does not matter

Hike up a mountain by the short trail or the long trail, and your height gain is the same. Enthalpy works the same way. That makes it a state function: it depends only on where you start and where you end.

If reactions add up to your target, their $\Delta H$ values add up to the answer. That is Hess's law.

Here is the game. Tap a card to flip it. Pick a number to multiply it. Species on opposite sides cross out when you add the cards. Clear everything that is not in the goal, and you win.

Worked example: Hess's law on paper

Find $\Delta H$ for $\ce{C(s) + 2H2(g) -> CH4(g)}$. Use: (A) $\ce{C(s) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)}$, $-393.5$ kJ. (B) $\ce{H2(g) + 1/2 O2(g) -> H2O(l)}$, $-285.8$ kJ. (C) $\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) ->}$ $\ce{CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}$, $-890.3$ kJ.

  1. Look at the target. C is on the left, and A has it on the left. Keep A. $\Delta H = -393.5$ kJ.
  2. The target needs 2 $\ce{H2}$ on the left. Multiply B by 2. $\Delta H = 2(-285.8) = -571.6$ kJ.
  3. The target has $\ce{CH4}$ on the right, but C has it on the left. Flip C. $\Delta H = +890.3$ kJ.
  4. Add them. $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{O2}$ all cancel, leaving the target.
  5. Add the numbers: $-393.5 + (-571.6) + 890.3 = $ $-74.8$ kJ.

Formation enthalpies: a shortcut

Chemists keep one big table. The standard enthalpy of formation, $\Delta H_f^\circ$, is the $\Delta H$ for making 1 mole of a substance from its elements in their standard states. Elements in their standard states sit at zero. Everything else is measured up or down from them, like heights from sea level.

$\Delta H^\circ$ from formation enthalpies

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta H^\circ = \sum n\,\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) \\ -\sum n\,\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})\end{gathered}$$
$n$
the coefficient from the balanced equation.
$\Delta H_f^\circ$
from the table, in kJ/mol. For elements in their standard state, such as $\ce{O2(g)}$, it is 0.

Worked example: burning methane from the table

Find $\Delta H^\circ$ for $\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) ->}$ $\ce{CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}$.

  1. Products, each times its coefficient: $1(-393.5) + 2(-285.8) = -965.1$ kJ.
  2. Reactants, each times its coefficient. Oxygen is an element, so it counts as zero: $1(-74.8) + 2(0) = -74.8$ kJ.
  3. Products minus reactants. Watch the double negative: $-965.1 - (-74.8) = -965.1 + 74.8 = $ $-890.3$ kJ.

Bond enthalpies: pay to break, get paid to make

When there is no table entry, you can estimate. Imagine that the reaction happens in two stages. First, pull every reactant apart into atoms. That costs energy. Then snap the atoms together into products. That pays energy back. The energy of each bond is its bond enthalpy.

$\Delta H$ from bond enthalpies (an estimate)

$$\begin{gathered}\Delta H \approx \sum E(\text{bonds broken}) \\ -\sum E(\text{bonds formed})\end{gathered}$$

Exam trap: the two formulas subtract in opposite orders

With formation enthalpies, it is products minus reactants. With bond enthalpies, it is reactants minus products (broken minus formed). Both are correct. Bond enthalpies are positive energies you must pay, and formation enthalpies are heights from sea level. Also, count every bond: $\ce{CH4}$ has four C–H bonds, and each counts.

Worked example: a bond enthalpy estimate

Estimate $\Delta H$ for $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}$.

  1. Count the bonds broken: one N≡N and three H–H. $945 + 3(436) = 2253$ kJ paid in.
  2. Count the bonds formed: each $\ce{NH3}$ has three N–H, so two molecules have six. $6(391) = 2346$ kJ paid back.
  3. Broken minus formed: $2253 - 2346 = $ $-93$ kJ. The table value, from formation enthalpies, is $-92.2$ kJ. A very close estimate.

Combustion enthalpies: which fuel is best?

The enthalpy of combustion is $\Delta H$ for burning 1 mole of a substance. It is always negative for a fuel. Which fuel wins depends on the question you ask, and that surprises people.

Pocket card

  • $\Delta H = H_{\text{products}} - H_{\text{reactants}}$. Negative means exothermic. Positive means endothermic.
  • Reverse an equation: flip the sign. Multiply an equation: multiply $\Delta H$.
  • Hess's law: add reactions to get the target, then add their $\Delta H$ values.
  • Formation: $\Delta H^\circ = \sum n\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum n\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})$. Elements in their standard states count as zero.
  • Bonds: $\Delta H \approx \sum E(\text{broken}) - \sum E(\text{formed})$. Breaking costs, and making pays.
