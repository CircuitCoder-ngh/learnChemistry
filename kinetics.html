Reaction rates

Molecules have to crash hard enough and the right way round. Almost everything about reaction speed follows from that.

What it takes to react

Chemistry gives you what a reaction makes. Kinetics tells you how fast it gets there. The story starts with one real reaction in the air around you.

Only some collisions count. A reaction speeds up when more collisions per second clear the energy bar.

Exam trap: why heat speeds things up

"Molecules collide more often" is only the small part. The main reason is that a much larger fraction of collisions now has at least the activation energy. Watch the second readout rise faster than the first.

The energy hill

Why is there a bar at all? Old bonds must start breaking before new ones can form, and that costs energy up front. On a graph, the cost looks like a hill.

A catalyst is a tunnel through the hill. It costs nothing to use, and it comes out at the same place. I am personally in favor of tunnels.

The Arrhenius equation: temperature and $E_a$ set $k$

$$k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}$$ $$\ln\frac{k_2}{k_1} = \frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right)$$
$R$
$8.314\text{ J/(mol K)}$, so write $E_a$ in joules per mole
$T$
Always in kelvin

Rate laws

A rate law is the rule connecting concentration to speed. Its exponents are the orders. They answer one question: if I double this reactant, what happens to the rate?

The rate law

$$\text{rate} = k\,[\text{A}]^m[\text{B}]^n$$
$k$
The rate constant. It changes with temperature and catalysts, not with concentration.
$m,\ n$
The orders, found by experiment. The overall order is $m + n$.

Exam trap: orders are not coefficients

In $\ce{2N2O5 -> 4NO2 + O2}$ the coefficient is 2, yet the measured rate law is $\text{rate} = k[\ce{N2O5}]$, which is first order. Unless a question says the step is elementary, get orders from data, never from the balanced equation.

Worked example: the method of initial rates

Find the rate law and $k$ for $\ce{2NO + O2 -> 2NO2}$ from these runs.

  1. Compare runs 1 and 2. Only $\ce{NO}$ changes. It doubles, and the rate goes up by $\dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-4}}{2.5 \times 10^{-5}} = 4$ times. Since $2^m = 4$, the order in $\ce{NO}$ is $m = 2$.
  2. Compare runs 1 and 3. Only $\ce{O2}$ changes. It doubles, and the rate goes up by $\dfrac{5.0 \times 10^{-5}}{2.5 \times 10^{-5}} = 2$ times. Since $2^n = 2$, the order in $\ce{O2}$ is $n = 1$.
  3. Write the law: $\text{rate} = k[\ce{NO}]^2[\ce{O2}]$. The overall order is 3.
  4. Put run 1 in and solve for $k$: $k = \dfrac{2.5 \times 10^{-5}\text{ M/s}}{(0.010\text{ M})^2(0.010\text{ M})} = $ $25\text{ M}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}$.

First order and half-life

In a first-order reaction, the rate depends on how much is left, so it slows as it goes. The amount falls by the same fraction in each equal stretch of time. Half gone, then half of what is left, and so on.

First-order reactions

$$\ln[\text{A}]_t = -kt + \ln[\text{A}]_0$$ $$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k} = \frac{0.693}{k}$$

Worked example: how much is left?

$\ce{N2O5}$ decomposes by first-order kinetics with $k = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}\text{ s}^{-1}$ at some temperature. If $[\ce{N2O5}]_0 = 0.200$ M, find the half-life and the concentration after 1000 s.

  1. Half-life: $t_{1/2} = \dfrac{0.693}{5.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{s}^{-1}} = $ $1.4 \times 10^{3}$ s, about 23 minutes.
  2. Write the first-order equation: $\ln[\ce{N2O5}]_t = -kt + \ln[\ce{N2O5}]_0$.
  3. Work out $kt$. Seconds cancel: $(5.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \cancel{\text{s}^{-1}})(1000\ \cancel{\text{s}}) = 0.50$.
  4. Substitute: $\ln[\ce{N2O5}]_t = -0.50 + \ln(0.200) = -0.50 - 1.609 = -2.109$.
  5. Undo the log with $e^x$: $[\ce{N2O5}]_t = e^{-2.109} = $ $0.12$ M.

Exam trap: half-lives do not add up to zero

After two half-lives, you have one quarter left, not nothing. And only first-order half-lives ignore the starting amount. Zero-order and second-order half-lives both change as the concentration changes.

Pocket card

  • To react, molecules must collide, with at least $E_a$, in the right orientation.
  • Heat raises $k$, mostly because more collisions clear $E_a$. Higher concentration means more collisions but leaves $k$ alone.
  • A catalyst gives a lower-$E_a$ path. It is not used up, and $\Delta H$ stays the same.
  • $\text{rate} = k[\text{A}]^m[\text{B}]^n$. Orders come from data, not coefficients.
  • First order: $\ln[\text{A}]_t = -kt + \ln[\text{A}]_0$ and $t_{1/2} = 0.693/k$.
