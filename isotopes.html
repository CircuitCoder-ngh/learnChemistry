Three particles, three jobs

Each particle has exactly one job. Protons decide the element. Neutrons change the mass. Electrons change the charge. The story shows how, then you take over.

The two sums you need

$$\begin{gathered} A = Z + N \\ \text{charge} = \text{protons} - \text{electrons} \end{gathered}$$
$A$
Mass number: protons plus neutrons
$Z$
Atomic number: protons
$N$
Number of neutrons

Exam trap: losing electrons makes it positive

It sounds backward. A cation is positive because it has lost electrons, and the protons now outnumber them. An anion is negative because it has gained electrons. $\ce{Na+}$ has 11 protons and 10 electrons. $\ce{O^2-}$ has 8 protons and 10 electrons. Changing electrons never changes the element.

Worked example: particles in an ion

How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in $\ce{^{56}_{26}Fe^3+}$?

  1. Protons are the atomic number, the bottom number: 26 protons.
  2. Neutrons are mass number minus atomic number: $N = A - Z = 56 - 26 =$ 30 neutrons.
  3. Charge is protons minus electrons, so $+3 = 26 - e$. Rearrange: $e = 26 - 3 =$ 23 electrons.
  4. The answer is 26 protons, 30 neutrons, 23 electrons.

Isotopes: same element, different mass

Atoms of one element always have the same number of protons, but they can have different numbers of neutrons. These versions are called isotopes. They are twins that weigh differently.

Same protons, different neutrons: that is an isotope. Same chemistry, different mass.

Weighing atoms one at a time

How do we know the isotopes of an element and how common each one is? A mass spectrometer does it. It turns atoms into ions, speeds them up, and lets a magnet bend them. Lighter ions bend more, so each isotope lands in its own spot.

Why chlorine is 35.45

The number on the periodic table is not the mass of any one atom. It is the weighted average of all the isotopes, counted by how common they are. Think of the isotopes as weights on a see-saw. The average is where it balances.

Average atomic mass

$$\begin{gathered} \text{average mass} = \\ \sum (\text{fraction} \times \text{isotope mass}) \end{gathered}$$
Fraction
The percent abundance divided by 100, such as 75.76% becoming 0.7576
Isotope mass
The mass of that isotope in u

Worked example: average mass of chlorine

Chlorine is 75.76% chlorine-35 (mass 34.97 u) and 24.24% chlorine-37 (mass 36.97 u). What is its average atomic mass?

  1. Turn percents into fractions: $75.76\% \to 0.7576$ and $24.24\% \to 0.2424$. Check they add to 1.
  2. Multiply each fraction by its mass: $(0.7576)(34.97\text{ u}) = 26.49\text{ u}$ and $(0.2424)(36.97\text{ u}) = 8.96\text{ u}$.
  3. Add them: $26.49\text{ u} + 8.96\text{ u} =$ $35.45$ u.
  4. Check it makes sense. It is closer to 35 than to 37, because chlorine-35 is the common one.

Worked example: find the abundance

Copper has two isotopes, copper-63 (62.93 u) and copper-65 (64.93 u). Its average mass is 63.55 u. What percent is copper-63?

  1. Call the fraction of copper-63 $x$. The two fractions add to 1, so the fraction of copper-65 is $1 - x$.
  2. Write the average mass equation: $62.93x + 64.93(1 - x) = 63.55$.
  3. Multiply out: $62.93x + 64.93 - 64.93x = 63.55$.
  4. Combine the $x$ terms: $64.93 - 2.00x = 63.55$.
  5. Subtract 64.93 from both sides: $-2.00x = -1.38$.
  6. Divide by $-2.00$: $x = 0.69$, so copper-63 is $69\%$ and copper-65 is 31%.

Exam trap: mass number is not atomic mass

Mass number is a count of particles, so it is always a whole number and belongs to one isotope, such as 35. Atomic mass is an average for the whole element, such as 35.45 u. And no chlorine atom has a mass of 35.45 u. Every atom is either about 35 or about 37. In the same way, no family has 2.3 children.

Use the see-saw to sanity check any answer. The average must land between the isotopes, and it must lean toward the common one. If your boron average is 12, something went wrong. Boron has been nowhere near 12.

Pocket card

  • Atomic number $Z$ is the protons, and it sets the element. Mass number $A$ is protons plus neutrons.
  • Neutrons: $N = A - Z$. Electrons in an ion: protons minus charge.
  • Isotopes have the same protons and different neutrons. Cations lost electrons. Anions gained them.
  • Average atomic mass: add up (fraction × isotope mass). It always lands between the isotopes.
  • Finding abundance: use $x$ and $1 - x$ as the two fractions, then solve.
