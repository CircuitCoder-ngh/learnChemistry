Solids and crystals

Salt shatters, copper bends, ice melts in your hand, and diamond laughs at a hammer. It all depends on what holds the particles together.

  • Sort any solid into ionic, molecular, covalent network or metallic
  • Explain its properties from what holds its particles together
  • Count the atoms in simple cubic, body-centered and face-centered unit cells
  • Calculate density from a unit cell

Four kinds of solid

Most solids are crystalline: their particles sit in a neat repeating pattern. Glass and many plastics are amorphous: the particles are jumbled, like a frozen liquid. Crystalline solids come in four kinds. To tell them apart, ask two questions. What are the particles? What holds them together?

Stronger forces between particles mean a harder solid with a higher melting point. Whether it conducts depends on whether any charge is free to move.

Put them to the test

Look at the particles and each property makes sense. Pick a solid and a test, and watch what happens inside. Why does a hammer bend copper but shatter salt, when both are held together by strong forces?

A quick way to sort: metal plus nonmetal is ionic. Only nonmetals is molecular, unless it is one of the few network solids you just memorize: diamond, graphite, $\ce{SiO2}$ and $\ce{SiC}$. A metal element alone is metallic.

Exam trap: melting ice versus melting diamond

Melting a molecular solid breaks only intermolecular forces, so the molecules stay whole. Melting a covalent network solid must break real covalent bonds, because the whole crystal is one giant molecule. That is why ice melts at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and diamond needs about $3500\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

Unit cells: counting atoms in a box

A crystal is a repeating pattern, so you only need to describe one box of it and then stack that box in every direction. This box is the unit cell. The catch: atoms on the walls are shared with the neighboring boxes. So each one only partly belongs to your box.

Worked example: a formula from a unit cell

In a crystal of perovskite, Ca is at the corners, O is at the face centers, and Ti is at the body center. What is its formula?

  1. Count Ca: 8 corners, each $\tfrac18$. $8 \times \tfrac18 = 1$ Ca.
  2. Count O: 6 faces, each $\tfrac12$. $6 \times \tfrac12 = 3$ O.
  3. Count Ti: 1 at the center, all inside. $1 \times 1 = 1$ Ti.
  4. The ratio Ca : Ti : O is 1 : 1 : 3, so the formula is $\ce{CaTiO3}$. Check the charges: $+2 + 4 + 3(-2) = 0$.

Exam trap: a picture of 14 atoms is not 14 atoms

A face-centered cubic drawing shows 14 spheres, but only 4 atoms belong to the cell. Do not simply count spheres. Multiply each position by its share. For salt, the 4 $\ce{Na+}$ and 4 $\ce{Cl-}$ per cell give a 1 to 1 ratio, as they should.

Density from a unit cell

Density is mass over volume, so find both for one cell. Since the cell repeats forever, the density of one cell is the density of the whole crystal. Here is a real trick that scientists use: measure the edge with X-rays, then see which cell type gives the right density.

Density from a unit cell

$$d = \frac{\text{mass of cell}}{\text{volume of cell}} = \frac{Z \times M}{N_A \times a^3}$$

Here $Z$ is the number of atoms per cell, $M$ is the molar mass in g/mol, $N_A$ is Avogadro's number and $a$ is the edge length in cm.

Worked example: density of copper

Copper is face-centered cubic, with an edge of 361.5 pm. Calculate its density.

  1. Count the atoms per cell: face-centered, so $Z = 8 \times \tfrac18 + 6 \times \tfrac12 = 4$.
  2. Mass of the cell: $4\ \cancel{\text{atoms}} \times \dfrac{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \cancel{\text{atoms}}} \times \dfrac{63.55\text{ g}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = 4.221 \times 10^{-22}\text{ g}$.
  3. Change the edge to cm: $361.5\ \cancel{\text{pm}} \times \dfrac{10^{-10}\text{ cm}}{1\ \cancel{\text{pm}}} = 3.615 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm}$.
  4. Cube it, number and unit: $(3.615 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm})^3 = 4.724 \times 10^{-23}\text{ cm}^3$.
  5. Divide: $d = \dfrac{4.221 \times 10^{-22}\text{ g}}{4.724 \times 10^{-23}\text{ cm}^3} =$ $8.94\text{ g/cm}^3$. The measured value is 8.96, so the model works.

Worked example: from edge to atomic radius

Iron is body-centered cubic, with $a = 286.6$ pm. Find the radius of an iron atom.

  1. In a body-centered cell, atoms touch along the body diagonal, not along the edge. That diagonal has length $\sqrt{3}\,a$.
  2. It crosses a corner radius, a whole center atom, and another corner radius, so it spans $4r$: $\sqrt{3}\,a = 4r$.
  3. Rearrange: $r = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}\,a}{4} = \dfrac{1.732 \times 286.6\text{ pm}}{4} =$ $124.1\text{ pm}$.

Exam trap: convert before you cube

Change pm to cm first, using $1\text{ pm} = 10^{-10}\text{ cm}$, and then cube both the number and the power of ten. Forgetting to cube the $10^{-8}$ is the most common way to get an answer off by a huge factor. Also check where atoms touch: edge for simple, body diagonal for body-centered, and face diagonal for face-centered.

Diamond and graphite: same atoms, different solid

Both are pure carbon, and both are covalent network solids. Diamond bonds every atom to 4 others in all three directions, so there is no weak direction. Graphite bonds each atom to only 3 others, in flat sheets. Weak London forces hold the sheets together, and each atom has one electron left over that can move along the sheet.

Pocket card

  • Ionic: ions, high melting, brittle, conducts only when melted or dissolved. Molecular: molecules, low melting, soft.
  • Covalent network: a giant net of bonds, very high melting, very hard. Metallic: cations in a sea of electrons, conducts, bends.
  • Shares in a cell: corner $\tfrac18$, edge $\tfrac14$, face $\tfrac12$, center 1.
  • Atoms per cell: simple 1, body-centered 2, face-centered 4. Touching neighbors: 6, 8, 12.
  • Atoms touch at: $a = 2r$ (simple), $\sqrt3\,a = 4r$ (body), $\sqrt2\,a = 4r$ (face).
  • $d = \dfrac{ZM}{N_A a^3}$, with $a$ in cm. Use $1\text{ pm} = 10^{-10}\text{ cm}$.
