Line spectra and the Bohr atom

Every element glows with its own set of colors, like a fingerprint. The reason is that electrons can only stand on certain energy steps.

  • Explain why an element gives sharp lines instead of a rainbow
  • Calculate the energy and wavelength of any hydrogen jump
  • Tell emission from absorption
  • Say what Bohr got right and wrong, and what de Broglie and Heisenberg added

Every element has a fingerprint

Sprinkle a sodium salt in a flame and the flame turns bright yellow. Copper turns it blue-green. Fireworks use exactly this trick. Now look at the glow through a prism, which spreads light out by wavelength. You do not get a rainbow. You get a few sharp lines called a line spectrum.

Atoms give off only certain wavelengths. Each element has its own set, so a line spectrum identifies an element, just as a fingerprint identifies a person.

Bohr's idea: energy steps

Why only certain colors? In 1913, Niels Bohr made a bold guess about hydrogen, the simplest atom. Step through it.

Energy of level $n$ in hydrogen

$$E_n = -\frac{2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}}{n^2}$$
$n$
the level number: 1, 2, 3, and so on. Whole numbers only.
$E_n$
negative because the electron is held. $E = 0$ means it has just broken free.

The Rydberg equation: the wavelength of a jump

$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{n_{\text{low}}^2} - \frac{1}{n_{\text{high}}^2}\right)$$
$R$
Rydberg constant, $1.097 \times 10^{7}\text{ m}^{-1}$
$\Delta E$
photon energy: $\Delta E = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}\left(\dfrac{1}{n_{\text{low}}^2} - \dfrac{1}{n_{\text{high}}^2}\right) = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$

Worked example: the red line of hydrogen

An electron falls from $n = 3$ to $n = 2$. What wavelength of light comes out?

  1. The low level is 2 and the high level is 3: $\dfrac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\dfrac{1}{2^2} - \dfrac{1}{3^2}\right)$.
  2. Do the bracket: $\dfrac{1}{4} - \dfrac{1}{9} = 0.2500 - 0.1111 = 0.1389$.
  3. Multiply by $R$: $\dfrac{1}{\lambda} = (1.097 \times 10^{7}\text{ m}^{-1})(0.1389) = 1.524 \times 10^{6}\text{ m}^{-1}$.
  4. Flip it. This is the step people forget: $\lambda = \dfrac{1}{1.524 \times 10^{6}\text{ m}^{-1}} = 6.56 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}$.
  5. Convert to nanometers: $656$ nm. That is red, and it matches the red line in the tube.

Worked example: how much energy to remove the electron?

How much energy does it take to pull the electron out of a hydrogen atom in its ground state? Give J per atom and kJ per mole.

  1. Removing it means going from $n = 1$ to $n = \infty$, where $E = 0$.
  2. $\Delta E = E_{\text{final}} - E_{\text{initial}} = 0 - (-2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}) = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}$ per atom.
  3. Per mole: $2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1} = 1.31 \times 10^{6}\text{ J/mol}$.
  4. The answer is $1310$ kJ/mol. This is the ionization energy of hydrogen, and it matches experiment.

Exam trap: the rungs are not evenly spaced

The levels crowd together as $n$ grows. So 1 to 2 is a far bigger jump than 2 to 3, and 5 to 6 is tiny. "Falls by one level" says nothing about energy. Always use the formula.

Exam trap: the sign of $\Delta E$

$\Delta E = E_{\text{final}} - E_{\text{initial}}$. A fall gives a negative $\Delta E$, because the atom lost energy. A climb gives a positive one. The photon's energy is always positive, so it equals the size of $\Delta E$.

Emission and absorption

A fall gives light out. A climb takes light in. So the same gaps can show up two ways, as bright lines or as dark ones.

Astronomers read the dark lines in starlight to learn what stars are made of. Nobody has been to a star. I have barely been to the surface.

Where Bohr breaks, and what came next

Bohr's model nails hydrogen, but it is not the final story. Two more ideas push us from neat orbits to something stranger.

Matter waves, and the limit on knowing

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$$
$$\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \frac{h}{4\pi}$$
$m, v$
mass in kg and speed in m/s. With $h$ in J·s, $\lambda$ comes out in meters.
$\Delta x, \Delta p$
the uncertainty in position and in momentum, where momentum $p = mv$

Worked example: the wavelength of an electron

An electron with a mass of $9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg moves at $2.19 \times 10^{6}$ m/s. What is its wavelength?

  1. Remember that $1\text{ J} = 1\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$. So $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}$.
  2. Multiply the bottom: $mv = (9.109 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg})(2.19 \times 10^{6}\text{ m/s}) = 1.995 \times 10^{-24}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.
  3. Divide: $\lambda = \dfrac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{1.995 \times 10^{-24}} = 3.32 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}$.
  4. The answer is $332$ pm, about the size of an atom. That is why wave behavior matters for electrons but not for baseballs.

Pocket card

  • Atoms give line spectra, because electrons have only certain energy levels.
  • $E_n = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}/n^2$. Levels crowd together as $n$ rises. $n = 1$ is the ground state.
  • Fall: photon emitted. Climb: photon absorbed. $|\Delta E| = hc/\lambda$.
  • $1/\lambda = R(1/n_{\text{low}}^2 - 1/n_{\text{high}}^2)$, with $R = 1.097 \times 10^{7}\text{ m}^{-1}$. Do not forget to flip.
  • Hydrogen: falls to $n = 1$ are UV, falls to $n = 2$ are visible (656, 486, 434, 410 nm), falls to $n = 3$ are infrared.
  • de Broglie: $\lambda = h/mv$. Heisenberg: you cannot know exact position and momentum together. Orbits give way to orbitals.
