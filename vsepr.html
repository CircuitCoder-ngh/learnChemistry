VSEPR and molecular shapes

Electrons push each other away. That one fact decides the shape of nearly every small molecule.

  • Count the electron groups around a central atom
  • Name all eleven molecular shapes and their bond angles
  • Tell electron geometry apart from molecular geometry
  • Decide if a molecule is polar by adding its bond dipoles

Electron groups push apart

Lewis structures are flat, but molecules are not. To see the real shape, use one rule: electron groups repel each other, so they get as far apart as they can. Tie some balloons together and you can see it for yourself.

Nobody tells them where to go

This model has no list of shapes in it. It only has charges on a sphere that push each other. Add some, shake them and let them settle. Lone pairs push a little harder than bonds, just as they do in real molecules.

From Lewis structure to shape

Here is the procedure. Draw the Lewis structure, count the groups around the central atom, then ask where the atoms ended up. Watch how the same four groups can look like two very different shapes.

Electron geometry counts every group. Molecular geometry only counts where the atoms are. Lone pairs are there, but you do not name them.

Worked example: a shape with a lone pair

What is the molecular shape of $\ce{SF4}$?

  1. Count valence electrons: S has 6 and each F has 7, so $6 + 4(7) = 34$.
  2. Each F keeps 8 electrons (one bond and 3 lone pairs): $4 \times 8 = 32$. Left over: $34 - 32 = 2$, which is 1 lone pair on S.
  3. Count groups: 4 bonds + 1 lone pair = 5 groups, so the electron geometry is trigonal bipyramidal.
  4. Place the lone pair in an equatorial spot, where it has the fewest neighbors at 90°.
  5. The atoms left look like a child's seesaw: seesaw, with ideal angles of 90° and 120°, squeezed smaller by the lone pair.

Exam trap: a double bond is one group

$\ce{CO2}$ has 4 bonding pairs on carbon, but they all point in only 2 directions. It has 2 groups, so it is linear. Counting it as 4 groups and calling it tetrahedral is the most common wrong answer.

Lone pairs take up more room

A bonding pair is held in place by two nuclei. A lone pair is held by only one, so it spreads out and pushes harder. The bond angles close up to make room.

A lone pair is like someone taking up two seats on a bus. Everyone else squeezes together a little. There is one on every bus I have taken to the lab.

With 5 or 6 groups, the spots are not all equal, so where a lone pair sits matters. The rule is to minimize 90° repulsions. They are the closest, so they cost the most. Find the best spot yourself below.

Is the molecule polar?

Polar bonds are not enough. Each polar bond is an arrow pulling toward its more electronegative atom. To find out if the whole molecule is polar, add those arrows like vectors, tip to tail. If you end up back where you started, they cancel.

Worked example: same shape, different answer

Is $\ce{CHCl3}$ polar? Use electronegativities C = 2.55, H = 2.20 and Cl = 3.16.

  1. Shape: 4 groups, no lone pairs, so tetrahedral.
  2. C-Cl bonds: difference $3.16 - 2.55 = 0.61$, with the arrow pointing toward Cl.
  3. C-H bond: difference $2.55 - 2.20 = 0.35$, with the arrow pointing toward C.
  4. The fourth corner does not balance the other three. In $\ce{CCl4}$ it would. So the arrows do not add to zero: polar.

Exam trap: polar bonds do not make a polar molecule

$\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{CCl4}$, $\ce{PCl5}$ and $\ce{SF6}$ all have polar bonds and are all nonpolar, because their shapes are balanced. If all the outer atoms match and there are no lone pairs on the center, the molecule is nonpolar. A few shapes with lone pairs, $\ce{XeF2}$ and $\ce{XeF4}$, are balanced too.

This tool adds bond dipoles only, which gives the right polar or nonpolar verdict for typical exam molecules. Lone pairs also contribute in reality. That is why $\ce{O3}$ is slightly polar even though every atom is oxygen, and why $\ce{NF3}$ has a smaller dipole than $\ce{NH3}$.

Pocket card

  • Count groups: each lone pair or bond of any order is one group.
  • 2 linear (180°), 3 trigonal planar (120°), 4 tetrahedral (109.5°), 5 trigonal bipyramidal (90° and 120°), 6 octahedral (90°).
  • Shape names describe only the atoms. Lone pairs are there, but you do not name them.
  • Lone pairs push harder and squeeze the bond angles. In a trigonal bipyramid they go equatorial. In an octahedron two go opposite each other.
  • Polar molecule: bond dipoles add up to more than zero. Balanced shapes with matching outer atoms are nonpolar.
