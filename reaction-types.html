The five reaction types

Most reactions you will meet are one of five dances. Spot the dance and you can predict what comes out.

  • Name the five reaction types and their patterns
  • Classify any equation by looking at its shape
  • Predict products from reactants, then balance
  • Say when a pattern does not guarantee a reaction

The five dances

Think of atoms and groups as dancers. In every reaction, partners change, but there are only a few ways it tends to happen. Learn these shapes in letters first. Real formulas follow the same steps.

TypePatternLook for
Combination, or synthesis$\ce{A + B -> AB}$Only one product
Decomposition$\ce{AB -> A + B}$Only one reactant
Single displacement$\ce{A + BC -> AC + B}$An element and a compound on each side
Double displacement$\ce{AB + CD -> AD + CB}$Two compounds swapping partners
Combustionfuel + $\ce{O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{CO2 + H2O}$$\ce{O2}$ on the left, burning
Do not read the formulas first. Count the things on each side. One product, one reactant, or a loose element: that shape names the type.

Real reactions, real atoms

Now the same dances with real substances. Pick a type and a reaction, then drag the slider. Keep your eye on which atoms change partners, and on what you would see in the lab.

Exam trap: some reactions wear two hats

$\ce{2H2 + O2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{2H2O}$ is combination, since it has one product, and it is also combustion, since hydrogen burns in oxygen. Exams normally accept the type the question is pointing at. Neutralization, such as $\ce{HCl + NaOH}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{NaCl + H2O}$, is a double displacement with its own name.

Predicting products

Here is the real payoff. Given only the reactants, the pattern tells you the products. There is one catch: the pattern gives you the partners, but you must still write correct formulas from the ion charges, and only then balance.

Four steps to predict products

$\text{name the pattern} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{swap partners} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{fix formulas with charges} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{balance}$

Exam trap: formulas before coefficients

$\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ make $\ce{AlCl3}$ because the charges must cancel. The subscript 3 comes from the charges, not from what you started with. Students often copy the subscripts from the reactants and write something like $\ce{AlCl2}$. Fix each formula first. Only then balance with coefficients.

A pattern tells you what would form, not whether it will. Zinc pushes copper out of solution, but copper will not push out zinc. Whether a reaction really goes is the next chapter of the story.

Will it really happen?

Two of the five types need one more check, and each has its own lesson coming up.

  • Single displacement only goes if the lone element is more reactive than the one it replaces. You will find the ranking in the activity series, in oxidation and reduction.
  • Double displacement only goes if something leaves the solution: a solid, a gas, or water. You will use the solubility rules and net ionic equations to check.
  • Combustion gets its own full treatment in combustion equations.

Worked example: predict a single displacement

Predict the products of magnesium metal in hydrochloric acid, then balance.

  1. Pattern. An element plus a compound: single displacement, $\ce{A + BC}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{AC + B}$.
  2. Swap. Mg replaces the H in HCl, so the products are a magnesium chloride and hydrogen.
  3. Fix formulas. $\ce{Mg^2+}$ with $\ce{Cl-}$ gives $\ce{MgCl2}$. Hydrogen on its own is diatomic: $\ce{H2}$.
  4. Balance. $\ce{Mg + 2HCl}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{MgCl2 + H2}$. Check: Mg 1 and 1, H 2 and 2, Cl 2 and 2.

Worked example: predict a double displacement

Predict the products of $\ce{Pb(NO3)2}$ and $\ce{KI}$, then balance.

  1. Pattern. Two compounds: double displacement, $\ce{AB + CD}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{AD + CB}$.
  2. Swap. Lead pairs with iodide, and potassium pairs with nitrate.
  3. Fix formulas. $\ce{Pb^2+}$ with $\ce{I-}$ gives $\ce{PbI2}$. $\ce{K+}$ with $\ce{NO3-}$ gives $\ce{KNO3}$.
  4. Balance. $\ce{Pb(NO3)2 + 2KI}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{PbI2 + 2KNO3}$. The bright yellow solid that forms is $\ce{PbI2}$.

Pocket card

  • Combination: many make one. Decomposition: one makes many.
  • Single displacement: a lone element kicks another out of a compound.
  • Double displacement: two compounds swap partners. Cation stays first.
  • Combustion: fuel plus $\ce{O2}$ gives $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$.
  • To predict: name the pattern, swap partners, fix formulas with charges, then balance.
  • A pattern says what would form. The activity series and solubility rules say if it really goes.
