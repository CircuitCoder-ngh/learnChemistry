Batteries and electrochemistry

A battery is a redox reaction with one trick: the electrons have to take the long way, through a wire.

From redox to electricity

You met this reaction in oxidation and reduction: zinc hands electrons to copper ions. Here is how to make those electrons do some work on the way.

Electrons flow through the wire from the anode to the cathode. Ions flow through the salt bridge to keep each side neutral.

Remember An Ox, Red Cat: anode is oxidation, reduction is at the cathode. Those two facts hold for every cell ever built, so they are worth more than my whole burrow.

Where the voltage comes from

Every metal ion has a standard reduction potential, $E^\circ$. It measures how hard that ion pulls electrons in, compared with hydrogen ions at exactly 0 volts. The higher one wins and gets reduced. The lower one is forced to give up its electrons.

Think of a tug of war. The cell potential, $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$, is the gap between the two players. A bigger gap means a bigger push on the electrons.

Cell potential

$$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$$

Both numbers go in as reduction potentials, exactly as the table lists them. The subtraction does the flipping for you. These values are for standard conditions: 1 M ions, 1 atm gases, 25 °C. Tables can differ by a few hundredths of a volt.

Worked example: find the voltage

A cell uses zinc and silver electrodes. Find $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$. Use $E^\circ(\ce{Zn^2+}) = -0.76$ V and $E^\circ(\ce{Ag+}) = +0.80$ V.

  1. Find the cathode. Silver has the higher $E^\circ$, so $\ce{Ag+}$ is reduced. Silver is the cathode and zinc is the anode.
  2. Write the formula: $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$.
  3. Put the numbers in: $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = (+0.80\text{ V}) - (-0.76\text{ V})$.
  4. Subtracting a negative adds: $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = +1.56\text{ V}$. It is positive, so the cell runs on its own.

Exam trap: never multiply a potential

Balancing $\ce{Zn + 2Ag+ -> Zn^2+ + 2Ag}$ means doubling the silver half-reaction. Many students double its 0.80 V as well and get 2.36 V. Do not. Voltage is push per electron, like pressure. Twice as many electrons get the same push. The answer stays 1.56 V.

Will it run on its own?

A positive $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ means the reaction is spontaneous. To connect voltage with energy, multiply it by the charge moved. Here $n$ is the moles of electrons transferred, and $F$ is Faraday's constant, the charge on one mole of electrons: 96,485 C/mol.

Voltage and free energy (one volt is one joule per coulomb)

$$\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}$$

Worked example: free energy of the zinc and copper cell

Find $\Delta G^\circ$ for $\ce{Zn + Cu^2+ -> Zn^2+ + Cu}$, with $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 1.10$ V.

  1. Count the electrons moved: each zinc gives up 2, so $n = 2$ mol.
  2. Set it up: $\Delta G^\circ = -(2\ \text{mol})\left(96{,}485\ \dfrac{\text{C}}{\text{mol}}\right)\left(1.10\ \dfrac{\text{J}}{\text{C}}\right)$.
  3. Moles and coulombs cancel: $\Delta G^\circ = -2.12 \times 10^{5}\text{ J}$.
  4. Convert to kilojoules: $\Delta G^\circ = -212\text{ kJ}$. Negative, so it is spontaneous, which matches the positive voltage.

Real batteries

Real batteries replace the beakers with pastes and thin layers, but they run on exactly these parts. Stack cells in series and their voltages add up. A battery dies when its reaction has run down and the push reaches zero. A rechargeable one can be pushed back the other way.

BatteryVoltageWorth knowing
Alkaline (AA)About 1.5 VZinc is oxidized, manganese dioxide is reduced. Single use.
Lead-acid (car)About 12 VSix cells of about 2 V in series. Rechargeable.
Lithium-ion (phone)About 3.7 VLithium's very low $E^\circ$ gives a big push for its weight. Rechargeable.

Running it backward

A battery lets a reaction run downhill and collects the energy. Electrolysis does the opposite: a power supply forces a reaction uphill. It is how we get sodium metal, aluminum, and a silver coat on a spoon.

Exam trap: the signs flip, the jobs do not

"The anode is negative" is only true in a battery. In electrolysis the anode is positive, because the power supply pulls electrons out of it. Go by the job instead. Oxidation is always at the anode, and reduction is always at the cathode.

How much metal do you get?

Electrons are the reactant you are paying for, so count them. Current in amperes is coulombs per second. Multiply by time for charge, divide by Faraday's constant for moles of electrons, and then use the half-reaction's mole ratio, just as in stoichiometry.

The electrolysis chain

$Q = I \times t$ $\text{mol } e^- = \frac{Q}{F}, \quad F = 96{,}485\ \text{C/mol}$

Worked example: silver plating

A current of 1.50 A runs through a silver nitrate solution for 25.0 minutes. What mass of silver plates out?

  1. Change time to seconds: $25.0\ \cancel{\text{min}} \times \dfrac{60\text{ s}}{1\ \cancel{\text{min}}} = 1500\text{ s}$.
  2. Find the charge: $Q = I \times t = \left(1.50\ \dfrac{\text{C}}{\cancel{\text{s}}}\right)(1500\ \cancel{\text{s}}) = 2250\text{ C}$.
  3. Find moles of electrons: $2250\ \cancel{\text{C}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol } e^-}{96{,}485\ \cancel{\text{C}}} = 0.02332\text{ mol } e^-$.
  4. Use the half-reaction $\ce{Ag+ + e- -> Ag}$, which is 1 electron per atom: $0.02332\text{ mol Ag}$.
  5. Change to grams: $0.02332\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{107.87\text{ g}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = $ $2.52\text{ g Ag}$.

The same current plates less copper than silver, mole for mole. Copper needs 2 electrons per atom and silver needs only 1. Check the half-reaction before you touch your calculator.

Pocket card

  • An Ox, Red Cat: oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode, always.
  • Electrons go through the wire, anode to cathode. Ions go through the salt bridge: anions toward the anode, cations toward the cathode.
  • $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$. Never multiply $E^\circ$ when you balance.
  • Positive $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ means spontaneous, and $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ comes out negative.
  • Battery: anode negative. Electrolysis: anode positive and a power supply does the pushing.
  • Metal plated: amps × seconds, then ÷ 96,485, then the electron ratio, then × molar mass.
