On every exam

Energy and heat

Heat is energy on the move. Its sign tells you which way it went, and that one sign wins or loses a lot of exam points.

  • Tell kinetic energy from potential energy at the particle level
  • Draw the system and surroundings, and give $q$ the right sign
  • Explain why a warm bathtub beats a hot teacup
  • Solve $q = mc\Delta T$ for any variable

Two kinds of energy

Energy is the ability to do work or to move heat. It comes in two main flavors: energy of motion and energy of position. Rocks have both. So do atoms.

Energy is never created or destroyed. It only moves around or changes form. This is the law of conservation of energy, also called the first law of thermodynamics.

Exam trap: breaking bonds does not release energy

You may have heard that energy is "stored in bonds" and comes out when they break. It is backward. Breaking a bond always costs energy, like pulling two magnets apart. Forming a bond always releases energy. A fuel gives off heat because the bonds it forms are stronger than the bonds it breaks.

System and surroundings

To talk about heat, first draw a line around the thing you care about. Inside the line is the system. Everything else is the surroundings. Every sign in this unit is written from the point of view of the system.

Heat leaving the system is negative: exothermic. Heat entering the system is positive: endothermic.

Heat lost by one side is heat gained by the other

$$q_{\text{system}} = -\,q_{\text{surroundings}}$$
$q$
heat, in joules. The sign shows the direction.
$q < 0$
exothermic: heat leaves the system, and the surroundings warm up.
$q > 0$
endothermic: heat enters the system, and the surroundings cool down.

Exam trap: the thermometer sits in the surroundings

If the water around a reaction gets hotter, the reaction is exothermic and its $q$ is negative. Students see the temperature climb and write a positive sign. The water's $q$ is positive, but the question asked about the reaction. Always ask: whose $q$ is this?

Heat is not temperature

Temperature measures how fast the particles are moving on average. Thermal energy is the total kinetic energy of all of them. Heat is thermal energy that moves from hot to cold. A teacup can be hotter than a bathtub and still hold far less thermal energy, because the tub has far more particles.

Spill a teacup on your lap and you yelp. Sit in a warm tub and you relax. But only one of them could melt 75 kg of ice, and it is the one I keep my rubber duck in.

Specific heat: why some things heat up fast

A metal bench in the sun gets too hot to sit on. The lake next to it stays cool. They both got the same sunshine. Some materials simply need more heat to warm up. The heat needed to warm 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C is its specific heat, $c$.

The heat equation

$$q = m\,c\,\Delta T$$
$q$
heat, in joules (J). Positive when the sample warms, negative when it cools.
$m$
mass, in grams (g).
$c$
specific heat, in J/g·°C.
$\Delta T$
$T_{\text{final}} - T_{\text{initial}}$, in °C. A change of 1 °C is the same size as a change of 1 K.

Exam trap: final minus initial, always

$\Delta T$ is always final minus initial. Cooling from 80 °C to 30 °C gives $\Delta T = -50$ °C, and that minus sign is what makes $q$ negative. Do not subtract the small number from the big one out of habit.

Worked example: finding heat

How much heat warms 250. g of water from 20.0 °C to 75.0 °C?

  1. Find the change, final minus initial: $\Delta T = 75.0 - 20.0 = +55.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
  2. Write the equation and fill it in: $q = mc\Delta T = (250.\ \text{g})(4.184\ \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}})(55.0\ {}^\circ\text{C})$.
  3. Grams cancel and °C cancels, leaving joules: $q = 57{,}530\ \text{J}$.
  4. Round to three significant figures and convert: $q = +57.5$ kJ. It is positive, so heat entered the water.

Solving for anything

Exams love hiding the unknown. They may give $q$ and ask for the mass, or the final temperature. It is always the same equation, rearranged. Divide both sides by whatever is keeping your unknown company.

Worked example: a cooling block

A 50.0 g iron block at 120.0 °C loses 1.35 kJ of heat. What is its final temperature? Use $c = 0.45$ J/g·°C.

  1. Give the heat its sign and put it in joules. The block loses heat, so $q = -1350$ J.
  2. Rearrange for $\Delta T$ by dividing both sides by $mc$: $\Delta T = \dfrac{q}{mc}$.
  3. Fill it in. Joules and grams cancel, leaving °C: $\Delta T = \dfrac{-1350\ \text{J}}{(50.0\ \text{g})(0.45\ \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}})} = -60\ {}^\circ\text{C}$.
  4. Use $\Delta T = T_f - T_i$, so $T_f = T_i + \Delta T = 120.0 + (-60)$. The answer is $T_f = 60\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

Worked example: identify the mystery metal

A 75.0 g metal bar absorbs 1.30 kJ and warms from 20.0 °C to 58.6 °C. Which metal is it most likely to be?

  1. Find the change: $\Delta T = 58.6 - 20.0 = +38.6\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Put the heat in joules: $q = +1300$ J.
  2. Rearrange for $c$ by dividing both sides by $m\Delta T$: $c = \dfrac{q}{m\Delta T}$.
  3. Fill it in: $c = \dfrac{1300\ \text{J}}{(75.0\ \text{g})(38.6\ {}^\circ\text{C})} = 0.449\ \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}}$.
  4. Look it up in the table. The answer is iron, which has $c$ of about 0.45.

Energy units

The SI unit of energy is the joule (J). Reactions involve thousands of joules, so chemists often use kilojoules (kJ). Older books use the calorie (cal), and food labels use a third one that trips everybody up.

Energy conversions

$$\begin{gathered}1\text{ kJ} = 1000\text{ J} \\ 1\text{ cal} = 4.184\text{ J} \\ 1\text{ Cal} = 1000\text{ cal} = 1\text{ kcal}\end{gathered}$$

Exam trap: joules, kilojoules, and that capital C

Specific heat uses joules, but many answers come out in kilojoules. Convert before you mix them. And a food Calorie, with a capital C, is 1000 ordinary calories. A 90 Cal snack is 90,000 cal, or about 377 kJ.

Pocket card

  • Kinetic energy is motion. Potential energy is position or attraction. Total energy never changes.
  • The system is what you are studying. The surroundings are everything else.
  • Exothermic: heat leaves, $q < 0$, surroundings warm. Endothermic: heat enters, $q > 0$, surroundings cool.
  • Temperature is average particle motion. Heat is energy moving from hot to cold.
  • $q = mc\Delta T$, with $\Delta T$ as final minus initial. For water, $c = 4.184$ J/g·°C.
  • 1 cal = 4.184 J. 1 kJ = 1000 J. 1 food Cal = 1000 cal.
