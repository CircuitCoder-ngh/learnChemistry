Solutions and molarity

Stir salt into water and it seems to vanish. It is still there, and you can count it.

  • Explain why water pulls salt apart, and why sugar stays whole
  • Sort substances into strong, weak and nonelectrolytes
  • Calculate molarity, then use it to find moles, grams or volume
  • Dilute a solution, and find the concentration of each ion

What really happens when salt dissolves

A solution is an even mixture of two or more substances. The part that gets dissolved is the solute. The part that does the dissolving is the solvent. When water is the solvent, we call it an aqueous solution and write $\ce{(aq)}$.

Water is polar. Its charged ends pull ionic compounds apart and wrap around each ion.

Now you control it. Drag the slider to dissolve the crystal, then switch to sugar and compare what leaves the crystal.

Exam trap: dissolving is not the same for everything

Ionic compounds split into ions: $$\begin{gathered}\ce{NaCl(s) ->} \\ \ce{Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}\end{gathered}$$ Most molecular compounds stay whole: $$\begin{gathered}\ce{C12H22O11(s) ->} \\ \ce{C12H22O11(aq)}\end{gathered}$$ Never write sugar as ions. And the salt has not disappeared: evaporate the water and you get it all back.

Which solutions carry electricity?

Moving charges carry current. Ions in water can move, so a solution with ions can complete a circuit. A substance that gives ions in water is an electrolyte. Test some. Can you make a weak electrolyte shine as brightly as a strong one by making it more concentrated?

Exam trap: strong does not mean concentrated

"Strong" and "weak" are about what fraction splits into ions. "Concentrated" and "dilute" are about how much is dissolved. You can have a dilute strong acid or a concentrated weak acid. Pure water is a very poor conductor too, so a shock in the bath comes from the ions dissolved in it.

Molarity: how crowded is it?

You need a way to say how concentrated a solution is. Chemists count the moles of solute in each liter of solution. That number is the molarity, written with a capital M and said "molar".

Molarity

$$M = \frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{liters of solution}}$$
$M$
molarity, in mol/L, also written M
Rearranged
moles $= M \times$ liters, and liters $=$ moles $\div M$

Try to reach the same molarity two different ways. More solute in more water can match less solute in less water. Taste is not a valid lab method, even for chemists with excellent whiskers.

Worked example: finding molarity from grams

You dissolve 29.2 g of NaCl and add water until there is 250. mL of solution. What is the molarity?

  1. Change grams to moles. $29.2\ \cancel{\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ mol}}{58.44\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 0.500\text{ mol NaCl}$.
  2. Change mL to liters. $250.\ \cancel{\text{mL}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ L}}{1000\ \cancel{\text{mL}}} = 0.250\text{ L}$.
  3. Divide moles by liters. $M = \dfrac{0.500\text{ mol}}{0.250\text{ L}} = $ $2.00\text{ M}$.

Worked example: how much to weigh out

How many grams of $\ce{CuSO4}$ do you need to make 500. mL of 0.150 M solution?

  1. Change to liters: $500.\text{ mL} = 0.500\text{ L}$.
  2. Find moles. Liters cancel: $0.500\ \cancel{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{0.150\text{ mol}}{1\ \cancel{\text{L}}} = 0.0750\text{ mol}$.
  3. Find grams. $0.0750\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{159.62\text{ g}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = $ $12.0\text{ g CuSO}_4$.

Exam trap: liters of solution, not liters of water

Molarity uses the final volume of the solution. Adding 1.00 L of water to a mole of salt gives a bit more than 1.00 L of solution, so it is a bit less than 1.00 M. Also, always change mL to L first. Forgetting makes your answer 1000 times too small.

Dilution: adding water keeps the moles

Labs store concentrated stock solutions and water them down when needed. Adding water spreads the solute out, and it adds no solute at all. So the moles before equal the moles after. Can you hit the target concentration?

Dilution

$$M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2$$
$M_1, V_1$
concentration and volume of the stock
$M_2, V_2$
concentration and total volume after adding water
Why
$M \times V$ is moles, and the moles do not change. Use the same volume unit on both sides.

Worked example: dilution

How much 6.00 M $\ce{HCl}$ do you need to make 250. mL of 0.500 M $\ce{HCl}$? How much water do you add?

  1. Write what you know. $M_1 = 6.00$ M, $M_2 = 0.500$ M, $V_2 = 250.$ mL. You want $V_1$.
  2. Rearrange by dividing both sides by $M_1$: $V_1 = \dfrac{M_2 V_2}{M_1}$.
  3. Put in the numbers. $V_1 = \dfrac{0.500\ \cancel{\text{M}} \times 250.\text{ mL}}{6.00\ \cancel{\text{M}}} = $ $20.8\text{ mL}$ of stock.
  4. $V_2$ is the total, so the water is the difference: $250. - 20.8 = $ $229\text{ mL}$ of water. In real life, add acid to water, never water to acid.

Exam trap: $V_2$ is the total volume

If you start with 100 mL and add 300 mL of water, then $V_2$ is 400 mL, not 300 mL. Read carefully: does the question ask for the final volume, or for the water to add?

How many ions are in there?

A strong electrolyte breaks into several ions, so each ion can have a different concentration from the compound. The subscripts tell you exactly how many.

Worked example: ion concentration

What are the concentrations of the ions in 0.150 M $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$?

  1. Write how it splits, keeping sulfate in one piece: $$\ce{Al2(SO4)3 -> 2Al^3+ + 3SO4^2-}$$
  2. Use the coefficients. $0.150\text{ M} \times 2 = $ $0.300\text{ M Al}^{3+}$.
  3. $0.150\text{ M} \times 3 = $ $0.450\text{ M SO}_4^{2-}$.

Solution stoichiometry: a new door to the tunnel

Reactions in water use the same map you learned in stoichiometry. Molarity just adds another door into the mole tunnel: liters times molarity gives moles.

Worked example: volume of a reacting solution

What volume of 0.100 M $\ce{AgNO3}$ reacts completely with 25.0 mL of 0.200 M $\ce{CaCl2}$? The reaction is $$\ce{CaCl2 + 2AgNO3 -> 2AgCl + Ca(NO3)2}$$

  1. Go in through the door: find moles of $\ce{CaCl2}$. $0.0250\ \cancel{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{0.200\text{ mol}}{1\ \cancel{\text{L}}} = 0.00500\text{ mol CaCl}_2$.
  2. Go across with the mole ratio. $0.00500\ \cancel{\text{mol CaCl}_2} \times \dfrac{2\text{ mol AgNO}_3}{1\ \cancel{\text{mol CaCl}_2}} = 0.0100\text{ mol AgNO}_3$.
  3. Come out through the other door. Moles over molarity gives liters. $0.0100\ \cancel{\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{1\text{ L}}{0.100\ \cancel{\text{mol}}} = 0.100\text{ L} = $ $100.\text{ mL}$.

Pocket card

  • The solute dissolves in the solvent to make a solution. Polar water surrounds ions: O ends face cations, H ends face anions.
  • Strong electrolytes split completely, weak ones barely, and nonelectrolytes not at all.
  • Molarity: $M = \text{mol} \div \text{L of solution}$. Change mL to L first.
  • Dilution: $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$, where $V_2$ is the total volume. Moles stay the same.
  • Ion concentration: multiply by the number of each ion in the formula. Polyatomic ions stay whole.
  • In reactions: $\text{L} \times M = \text{mol}$. Then use the mole ratio as usual.
