On every exam

Meet the periodic table

It is a seating chart. Elements in the same column behave alike, so one spot tells you a lot.

  • Read a tile, and find any element's group and period
  • Sort elements into metals, nonmetals and metalloids
  • Name the main families and say how each one behaves
  • Predict an ion's charge from its group, and list the diatomic elements

Why does it have this odd shape?

In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev was writing a textbook and wanted a way to organize the elements. He wrote them on cards and started sorting. What he found turned a list into a map.

Properties repeat in a pattern. That is what "periodic" means, and it is why each column is a family.

Reading a tile

Every square holds the same few facts. Learn to read one, and you can read all 118.

Explore the table

Tap any element to inspect it. Then switch views. Can you find the only two elements that are liquid at room temperature? Can you spot the shape made by the diatomic elements?

Exam trap: hydrogen is not an alkali metal

Hydrogen sits at the top of group 1 because it has one outer electron, but it is a nonmetal gas. It is not a member of the alkali metal family. Also: helium sits in group 18 with the noble gases, even though it has only 2 outer electrons.

Metals, nonmetals, and the ones in between

Most elements are metals: shiny, bendable, and good at carrying electricity. Nonmetals, in the upper right, are usually dull, brittle and poor conductors. Metalloids sit on the staircase between them, with properties of both. Test some samples and decide for yourself.

Your group predicts your charge

Noble gases have full outer shells and rarely react. Atoms nearby lose or gain electrons to match them. So the group number tells you the charge of the ion, and you do not need to memorize it.

Quick rule for main group elements. Metals on the left: the charge matches the group, so group 1 is $+1$, group 2 is $+2$, group 13 is $+3$. Nonmetals on the right: count back from 18, so group 17 is $-1$, 16 is $-2$, 15 is $-3$.

Seven elements that never go out alone

Most elements are written as single atoms: $\ce{Fe}$, $\ce{C}$, $\ce{He}$. But seven of them always travel in pairs when they are on their own.

Exam trap: oxygen gas is $\ce{O2}$, never $\ce{O}$

Whenever one of these seven elements appears on its own in an equation, write the pair: $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{F2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{Br2}$, $\ce{I2}$. In a compound, the 2 disappears: $\ce{H2O}$ has only one O. Remember them as HONClBrIF, said "honkle-brif".

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • The table runs in order of atomic number. Columns are groups, rows are periods.
  • Elements in a group behave alike, because they have the same number of outer electrons.
  • Metals are on the left, nonmetals on the upper right, metalloids on the staircase.
  • Group 1 alkali metals, group 2 alkaline earths, group 17 halogens, group 18 noble gases.
  • Ion charges: groups 1, 2, 13, 15, 16, 17 give $+1, +2, +3, -3, -2, -1$. Transition metals vary.
  • Diatomics: HONClBrIF.
