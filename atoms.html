Often tested

Inside the atom

An atom is almost entirely empty space. You are about to prove it by firing particles at gold.

  • Use the laws of conservation of mass, definite proportions and multiple proportions
  • Say what Thomson, Millikan and Rutherford each discovered
  • Give the mass, charge and location of the proton, neutron and electron
  • Explain how gold foil proved that atoms are mostly empty

Three clues that atoms exist

Nobody could see an atom in 1800. But careful weighing left clues. Step through them, then see what John Dalton made of them.

Law of conservation of mass

$$\begin{gathered} \text{total mass of reactants} \\ = \text{total mass of products} \end{gathered}$$

Worked example: conservation of mass

In a sealed flask, 12.0 g of carbon burns in oxygen and makes 44.0 g of carbon dioxide. Nothing is left over. How much oxygen reacted?

  1. Write the law for this reaction: mass of carbon + mass of oxygen = mass of carbon dioxide.
  2. Put in the numbers: $12.0\text{ g} + m = 44.0\text{ g}$.
  3. Subtract 12.0 g from both sides: $m = 44.0\text{ g} - 12.0\text{ g}$.
  4. The answer is $32.0$ g of oxygen.

Worked example: multiple proportions

Nitrogen and oxygen form two compounds. For every 1.00 g of nitrogen, one holds 0.57 g of oxygen and the other holds 1.14 g. Show they obey the law of multiple proportions.

  1. Fix the mass of nitrogen. It is already 1.00 g in both, so compare only the oxygen.
  2. Divide the larger oxygen mass by the smaller: $\dfrac{1.14\ \cancel{\text{g}}}{0.57\ \cancel{\text{g}}} = 2.0$.
  3. The ratio is 2 to 1, small whole numbers. The formulas could be $\ce{N2O}$ and $\ce{NO}$: one has twice as much oxygen per nitrogen.

Thomson finds something smaller

In 1897 J. J. Thomson sent electricity through a glass tube with almost all the air pumped out. A beam shot from the negative metal plate, the cathode, so he called it a cathode ray. Now you run the tube.

In 1909 Robert Millikan timed tiny oil drops falling between charged plates and measured the charge of one electron: $-1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C. With Thomson's ratio, that gives its mass: $9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg. Extremely light. I have eaten heavier crumbs.

The gold foil surprise

Atoms are neutral, so if electrons are negative, something positive must be in there too. But where? In 1909 Ernest Rutherford asked Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden to find out.

An atom has a tiny, heavy, positive center called the nucleus. Everything else is mostly empty space.

Now fire it yourself

The tube starts with the model everyone believed. Watch the screen for a while. Then flip to the nuclear model and see what changes. Can you bounce one straight back at the gun?

Exam trap: what actually bounced the alphas?

Not the electrons. An alpha particle is about 7,300 times heavier than an electron, so electrons barely nudge it, like a bowling ball hitting a ping-pong ball. Only something heavy and strongly positive can turn an alpha around. That is the nucleus.

How empty is an atom?

Pictures of atoms lie, because a true-scale nucleus would be too small to draw. So let's fly in and find it. If the nucleus were a marble on the center spot, the atom would be a whole stadium.

The three particles

The positive particles in the nucleus are protons. In 1932 James Chadwick found the neutral ones, neutrons. Add the electrons in the cloud and you have all three.

Exam shortcut: a proton and a neutron each weigh about 1 u, and an electron weighs about 0 u. But how light is "about 0"? Find out with the balance.

Exam trap: who found what

These get swapped on tests. Thomson: the electron (cathode rays, 1897). Millikan: the electron's charge (oil drops, 1909). Rutherford: the nucleus (gold foil, 1909 to 1911). Chadwick: the neutron (1932). Thomson did not find the nucleus. His model did not have one.

A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, because their charges cancel. It does not need equal protons and neutrons. Gold has 79 protons, 79 electrons, and 118 neutrons.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Conservation of mass: total mass before equals total mass after.
  • Definite proportions: a compound always has the same mass ratio. Multiple proportions: two compounds of the same elements give small whole-number ratios.
  • Thomson found the electron. Millikan measured its charge. Rutherford found the nucleus. Chadwick found the neutron.
  • Gold foil: most alphas go straight (mostly empty), a few bend (positive center), rare ones bounce back (tiny and heavy center).
  • Proton: +1, about 1 u, nucleus. Neutron: 0, about 1 u, nucleus. Electron: −1, about 0 u, cloud.
