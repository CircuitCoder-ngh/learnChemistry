Often tested

Ionic bonds

A metal hands an electron to a nonmetal. Now one is positive, one is negative, and they stick together hard.

  • Show how electrons move to form ions, in pictures and Lewis dots
  • Write the formula of an ionic compound from its ion charges
  • Use Coulomb's law to rank lattice energies
  • Explain why ionic solids melt hot, shatter, and conduct only when molten

Give and take

Metals hold their outer electrons loosely. Nonmetals pull hard on them. Put the two together and an electron moves all the way across. This is electron transfer, and it makes two ions.

An ionic bond is not shared electrons. It is the pull between a positive ion and a negative ion.

Who gives, who takes

Group number tells you how many electrons each atom gives or takes. Sometimes one atom's electrons are not enough, so more atoms join in until all the electrons have somewhere to go.

Worked example: a formula from charges

What compound forms from aluminum and oxygen?

  1. Al is in group 13, with 3 valence electrons. It loses all 3: $\ce{Al^{3+}}$.
  2. O has 6 valence electrons. It gains 2 to reach 8: $\ce{O^{2-}}$.
  3. Charges must add to zero. The smallest number both 3 and 2 go into is 6.
  4. So 2 Al give $2 \times 3 = 6$ electrons, and 3 O take $3 \times 2 = 6$ electrons.
  5. The formula is $\ce{Al2O3}$, and the charges add to $+6 - 6 = 0$.

Who pays for it?

Here is a surprise that many students never hear. Moving an electron from Na to Cl actually costs energy. So why does salt form at all? Follow the energy.

Exam trap: not just because of the octet

"Sodium loses an electron because it wants an octet" is not the whole story. Forming the ions from atoms costs energy overall. The lattice energy, released when the ions pack together, is what makes ionic compounds stable. Also watch the sign: some books list lattice energy as positive, for pulling the crystal apart.

Coulomb's law

How hard do two ions pull on each other? Only two things matter: how big their charges are and how far apart they are. Try it.

Coulomb's law for two charges

$$F \propto \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} \qquad E \propto \frac{q_1 q_2}{r}$$
$F$
Force of attraction between the ions
$E$
Energy of attraction, which is what lattice energy follows
$q_1, q_2$
Charges of the two ions
$r$
Distance between their centers, close to the sum of their radii

Worked example: rank two lattice energies

Which has the larger lattice energy, $\ce{MgO}$ or $\ce{NaCl}$? Use ionic radii $\ce{Mg^{2+}}$ 72 pm, $\ce{O^{2-}}$ 140 pm, $\ce{Na+}$ 102 pm, $\ce{Cl-}$ 181 pm.

  1. Write what energy depends on: $E \propto \dfrac{q_1 q_2}{r}$.
  2. $\ce{MgO}$: $\dfrac{(2)(2)}{72 + 140} = \dfrac{4}{212} = 0.0189$.
  3. $\ce{NaCl}$: $\dfrac{(1)(1)}{102 + 181} = \dfrac{1}{283} = 0.00353$.
  4. Compare: $\dfrac{0.0189}{0.00353} = 5.4$. The answer is $\ce{MgO}$, about 5 times larger. Measured values agree: about 3800 and 790 kJ/mol.

Try that reasoning in a duel. Always check the charges first. Only look at sizes if the charges tie.

Inside the crystal

Each ion attracts every opposite ion near it. So the ions do not pair up. They build a lattice, a huge 3D pattern. There is no such thing as one NaCl molecule in a salt crystal. The formula only gives the ratio.

One grain of table salt holds around $10^{18}$ formula units. Every ion in there is pulling on all of its neighbors. No wonder salt melts at 801 °C and my soup never argues.

Hard, hot and brittle

Every property of an ionic solid comes from the same fact: strong attractions between ions locked in place. A strong lattice is hard to melt, and $\ce{MgO}$ melts near 2800 °C. But hit it and something strange happens.

Exam trap: what carries the current

In molten or dissolved salt, moving ions carry the current, not free electrons. And solid salt has no shortage of charges: it just cannot move them. The ions are fixed in place until the lattice breaks down.

Check yourself

Pocket card

  • Metals lose electrons to become cations, and nonmetals gain them to become anions. Cations shrink and anions grow.
  • Charges must add to zero: that sets the formula.
  • $F \propto q_1 q_2 / r^2$ and $E \propto q_1 q_2 / r$. Rank lattice energies by charge first, then by size.
  • $\ce{NaCl}$ is a ratio in a lattice, not a molecule. Each ion has 6 neighbors of the other kind.
  • Hard and high-melting, but brittle. Conducts only when molten or dissolved.
